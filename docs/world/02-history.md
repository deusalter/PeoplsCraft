# 2. The history

This chapter gives the past of the ground in the order it happened, then the sources that exist inside the world, where they disagree, and what the present would lack without each part of the past.

On this ground the people who can hunt a great feeder and live cannot be born. They can only arrive. Everything that was built here was built while people kept arriving, and everything that ran down ran down when they stopped.

Statements here are firm unless they are given as somebody's telling. No townsperson, item text, banner or Guide line may say the firm version. A mouth in the game says what its owner would say, gains what its owner would gain and leaves out what its owner would leave out. Section numbers beginning with 1 point to the ground chapter.

## 2.1 How time is reckoned

The living ground has no seasons and no year (1.3). The round of eight nights is the only calendar, and nobody has a reason to number the rounds. So nobody born here can say how long ago anything was. Householders reckon by forebears: a thing happened in a named forebear's time, or before the names begin. Arrivals bring years from their own worlds, and those years measure nothing here.

Every span below is a range, in generations of the born. The ranges are firm as ranges and no finer. If anyone in the game gives a number, the number is that person's guess.

| Time | How long | Who was on the ground |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before people | Far longer than everything after it together | Feeders and ordinary animals |
| The first comers | Ten to forty generations | Arrivals in numbers, and their born children |
| The hunting stops | Two to five generations | The last arrivals, growing old, and the born |
| The unhunted generations | Twenty to sixty generations | The born, fewer in each generation |
| The present | Less than one lifetime since the first arrival by the new way | The born, and arrivals again |

## 2.2 Before people

**The world that grew on the root.** The root was alive once and bore a world. That world ended in an earlier age, as the worlds of its age did, and the game gives that no cause and shows none of it. Nothing a mortal people makes lasts from one age of worlds into the next: no line of descent, no speech, no wall. If that world had peoples, nothing of theirs is left, and nobody on the ground knows whether it had any.

What is left of it is the islands (`dim:2`), their two metals, and the root itself: `bedrock` under the living ground, and the same wood as floor and roof of the hollow (`dim:1`). No dressed stone on the islands is from that world. `arena6` and `cache2` are first comers' work. No art or text places a ruin of the earlier world anywhere.

**The root lying dead.** For a span nobody can measure, the root lay dead with no people on it. At some point in that span the living ground came to lie on it. How is unknown to everyone on the ground and is not settled here.

Matter lying over the root set harder the nearer it lay to the wood and the longer it lay (1.6), and the ten hardnesses are the result. Feeders that died uneaten left lees in the ground, and it set into crystal. Geodes are found sealed in rock that nobody has ever opened, so lees was setting here before anyone came to boil it.

The feeders had their own order, by their three rules (1.2). Each host followed its strongest. Each strongest held ground, and weaker things stayed off it and fed where it allowed. The first comers broke that order, and it came back when the hunting stopped. The ground at the start of play is closer to this time than to the first comers'.

## 2.3 The first comers

**Who they were.** Arrivals. They came along the dead road while its far end was joined to living roads at a junction that pathfinders knew. They came for the wood, as cutting crews do on every world. The root could not be cut. They stayed for the ground, which was sound in every direction at a time when their own worlds were thinning.

Feeders in the road took a company then as they do now (1.4). A pathfinder knows where in a road a few people can walk with what they carry, and that is the whole of the trade. So the first comers came a few at a time behind a pathfinder, with their tools. Few pathfinders live through many trips. The way was held open by one pathfinder after another, each making a handful of crossings.

Each of them had walked the dead wood, so each had a way in it. Their children were born on the ground and had none. From the first generation there were two kinds of person here: those who had walked in, who fell and rose at a bed, and those born, who died where they were killed. The born of that time held hearths and worked the trades. Those who had walked in did the hunting. The division of labour that the players meet (1.5) was made then, inside households where the two kinds were parents and children.

**What they did.** They brought the ward with them and found that here it needed no core. They found what the strongest animal of a host yields when it is boiled down. Then they did across a wide country what a late crew can do at one stone (1.6, After the last). They baited the strongest of a host onto a killing floor, killed it, boiled it down, and had great stones held against everything weaker with the charge, one night at a time. A charge does not outlast the night, and the strongest of the next host moves onto empty ground, so the killing did not end.

They took the kinds in the order the metals force. Each hardness is cut only with the one before, and from `cinder` upward each is worked only with the remains of an earlier kill (1.6). That order is the Guide's order. With the remains of the kinds of `boss:3` and `boss:5` they raised gates, went into the hollow and onto the islands for the harder matter, and set stones there.

Three things follow, and none of them is in the householders' telling.

- Every host in the held country was broken all the time. Nobody ate the kills, so nothing was followed. Outside the reach of a held stone the low feeders were as hard as they have ever been. What the game shows after eight first kills was the ordinary state of the first comers' nights.
- The great stones were never all held at once. Bait is taken only at a stone nobody is driving. To get the next kill of a kind, some stone of that kind had to stand dark as a killing floor while others of the kind were held.
- There were no first kills. The Guide was made in this time as the order of work. It has no line about what a first killing lets loose, because in its makers' time no host was whole. Nobody had to hide anything for the Guide to be silent on it now.

The deep kind came up on every dark night through all of this time, since the shade-kind's strongest was being killed as often as it grew. The patterns show it: the bait for `boss:7` and the bait for `boss:8` both need `moon_sliver`, which only the deep kind carries.

**What is left of them.**

- The six kinds of great stone on the living ground, with their killing floors. They recur at 224, 288, 352, 384, 416 and 480 strides: wider as the host is stronger, as reach would fall.
- `altar4` in the hollow and `altar6` on the islands. Both recur at 192 strides, closer than the weakest kind above. Nobody can hold where the hour stands (1.6). These two were set to be baited and were never held. The first comers needed the remains of those two kinds for furnace beds, and their spacing is a hunter's convenience.
- A stone of the first kind near the mouth. It was set to cover the place where every newcomer and every load came out of the road.
- `wardstone`, their dressed stone: the pad and dais of `arena3`, the base under `altar8`, every `dungeon`, and `cache1`.
- The `dungeon`: three to five rooms of `wardstone` under solid rock, well below the level of the sea, with no way in from above. These were the working stores of hunting crews. A chest holds lees, iron gear, `lumenite`, sometimes a `warden_bell` made ahead and a `recall_stone`. A store was closed with no door for two reasons. On this ground a room is closed by putting one piece back. And a store draws. Only a person with a way could work at that depth, in caves where feeders are up at all hours.
- `cache1` and `cache2`: the same stores in the hollow and on the islands, holding a `spore_censer` or a `prism_lens` ready.
- The `village`: four to six houses round a well, in seven kinds of country. Each house holds a `bedroll` or a `workbench`. A house with a `bedroll` was the near end of somebody's way. The villages housed both kinds of person.
- The patterns: every bait, the two gate kits, the four forges built round great remains. The born still carry them.
- Two signs on the near side that they crossed. Growth of the islands took in one kind of green country, and the turf of the hollow took in some caverns of the deep rock (chapter 3). Both spread from places where gates stood.

Three things are absent, and builders should not place them. No stone on the ground carries words: the lines cut in housings and recall stones are there to be driven. No first comers' gate stands anywhere: a gate shuts when a piece is pulled from its near frame, and a shut gate leaves nothing behind. And no forge of theirs stands. As great lees ran short at the end, the remains were taken out of forges, benches and gates and boiled down for one more night each.

## 2.4 The way fails and the hunting stops

**The way.** The junction at the far end of the dead road failed. In the present age old junctions fail as the Fraying goes on. The far end of the first way stood on ground the Fraying reached. When that ground went, the join between the dead road and the living roads went with it. The dead road was unchanged, because the wood does not fail with the speech. The mouth on this ground was unchanged.

Nobody here saw anything. People stopped coming up at the mouth. Any pathfinder then on the ground knew one way, and the far end of it was gone. Whether any of them went down to look, and whether the road let them out anywhere, is unknown (2.8). With the last pathfinder went the knowledge of where this ground hangs on the Tree. The arrivals already here went on rising at their beds until age took them, since the way does nothing against age.

**The hunting.** The hunters were not replaced. Great charges were still needed every night, so the born went to the stones. They knew what they were. Every born person of that time had grown up beside people who rose after a killing, and had seen that the born did not. They hunted, and they died where they were killed. They went on longest against the weakest hosts, since those were the kills a person without a way had some hope of living through. The strongest kinds were given up first.

The stones of each kind went dark by one of two roads, and at any one stone nobody knows which came first. The stored great lees of the kind was used up, a night at a time. Or the strongest of the kind, unhunted now, grew past the kills that were in store, and by rule 1 a charge made from a lesser kill did not turn it. Feeders are drawn to remains of their own kind, so it came to the stones. As each kind was left alone, its hosts gathered behind a strongest again.

Then nobody went to a great stone. This was decided a hearth at a time, by people who had watched what going cost.

**The sand country.** One part of the born went further and gave up the pot altogether. They were the people of the sand, and they had a reason nobody else had. The burrowing kind passes deep under the ground and crosses any ward (rule 6). In sand country a house ward had never turned the kind that mattered most there. It had only drawn (rules 4 and 5). While great stones were held near them, that was bearable. Without them a pot was a lure and nothing more. They burned what they had stored and what they killed, and found that with no hunter troubling it the strongest burrower let nothing else come up on its sand. Whether they left their hearths while stones were still held or after the dark is disputed (2.8).

**What is left of this time.** The `ruin`. It is a small store on the surface, floored in `cobble`, `moss_stone` and `stone_bricks`, with a lamp on a pillar and no `wardstone` in it. It is the work of hunters who could not go under the rock: the born, storing on the surface. Its chest sometimes holds a `verdant_idol`, the bait for the weakest host. Every `ruin` has had its walls broken. Feeders broke some, sand-country people some, and householders short of lees some. The householders' telling leaves out the third.

The crews' stores under the rock were not opened again. The people who had stocked them did not come back, and the born did not go under. That is why a `dungeon` is full. A `warden_bell` in its chest is a bait somebody made and did not live to lay.

This is the oldest thing householders tell as something that happened to people: the hunters were lost at the stones.

## 2.5 The unhunted generations

With nobody hunting them, the hosts were whole. Each strongest lay at the stones of its own smell and held its ground, and lesser things stayed off that ground and fed where it allowed. The low feeders lost the hardness that comes of eating leaderless packs. The nights became easier than the first comers had ever had them. A pot of lees from small kills and one hand on a hearthstone was enough for a house.

The born lived under the order of the great feeders. No pack went for their stores, because the strongest of the woodland host was alive. The deep kind never reached the surface, because the strongest of the shade-kind ate it below. The sand was empty of every lesser thing, because the burrower let nothing in. The sand-country people lived on those terms knowingly and paid for them in people.

Householders came to know it in their own way. They could see that a country with a great animal lying at its dark stones had quiet nights. They said the forebears had set each one over its country. Some of them began to lay small kills at the dark stones to keep the animal lying there, and it worked, since carrion at a spent stone is what brought it. That is where the words altar and offering come from (5.2). The baits of the Guide were called offerings afterwards by the same word.

The lees for the pots still had to be found. Some came from small kills, made by day or at the edge of a hearth's reach, and a born person who misjudges one dies of it. Some came from the first comers' salted stock, out of stores on the surface. Both sources were smaller in every generation. A hearth that could not be charged was left. A holder who died before anyone had learned at their stone left a hearth with nobody to sit at it. The villages emptied a hearth at a time, and no one generation saw it happen. Householders took to walking by day between the old houses and passing the nights in them.

Nothing was built. Not one structure on the ground belongs to this time.

The trades went on. The born carried the order of work, the patterns for baits none of them laid, and the cutting of stones for a way none of them had: `wayfinder` and `runescribe` both sell a `recall_stone` today.

In the old stores, salt failed in some room of nearly every one. Feeders came to the unsalted lees and have bred there since: the `brood_nest`. Moss took the surface stone. The spent great stones became the places where the strongest lie, and the stone-eating kind ate into the bases of the eighth kind of stone (4.10).

What is left of this time is the size of the great feeders, the nests, the empty villages with each `crate` stocked, and the quiet of the first nights in any new world.

## 2.6 The present

**The new way.** A tear. Cutting crews on many worlds work in dead remains. As the Fraying goes on, new ways tear open through such remains. At least one tear now opens from workings that crews already use into the road through this root. Nobody made it. It joins no living road. No pathfinder knows it, since the crews that find it are the crews that have lost theirs. It cannot be walked back: the order of things is worse damaged in the road than anywhere a gate reaches, and no two arrivals describe the same way in. They cannot tell from each other's accounts whether there is one tear or several, or whether they set out from the same world.

The first way brought people on purpose, behind a pathfinder, with their tools. The new one brings lost people one at a time, long apart, with nothing. The first comers needed nothing from anyone on the ground, because nobody was on it. The present arrivals need the born for the order of work, the trades and the first lees.

**What is happening.** The first comers' arrangement is being taken up again by two parties, neither of which knows it was ever in use. Householders supply the order of work and hold the hearths. Arrivals hunt. Before the crew the game follows, arrivals by the tear were few and scattered and none took a great stone, so at the start of play every host is whole. Each first killing ends the quiet for one kind: the host breaks, everything hardens, and the ground that animal held lies open. The crew is making the first comers' nights again, one kind at a time, in a country that has had easy nights for longer than anyone can tell.

After the last first killing the crew has the strongest kill there is, and a great stone could be held with it (1.6). From then on the present stands where the first comers stood, with one difference. The way that brings it people is a tear, and nobody knows how long a tear stays open.

## 2.7 The sources

### The stonework

It shows that the builders used beds and stones that serve only a person with a way. It shows which hosts they hunted, that they crossed into both older places, and that they stored for hunters: beside its lees a deep store holds gear, a bait made ahead and a stone for the way.

It misleads by what lasts. Dressed stone outlasts everything else, so the stonework shows the first comers at full strength and shows nothing of how their time ended. It shows the great stones as they stand now, all dark, and cannot show which were held and which were killing floors on a given night. The even spacings look like a single plan. They are what reach produces when stones of one kind are set to cover a country.

### What householders tell

`wayfinder` has the fullest telling, and the Guide is its working part. The other householders each carry the piece that belongs to a trade.

It is right about the order of work, the patterns for the baits, that great stones were held and turned whole hosts, and that hunters died at the stones.

It is wrong about who the builders were: it makes them born people of this ground. It is wrong that all the stones were lit at once, and that the nights were good while they were lit. It takes the quiet of the unhunted generations for the last of the forebears' work, done by great animals the forebears set over each country.

It came to run that way without anyone lying. It is the telling of the lines that lasted, and those are the lines whose forebears stayed at the hearth. A hearth that sent its people to the stones ended, and its telling ended with it. So the hunters come down as people who were lost, told by the descendants of those who did not go. It also serves a present need. The born want great lees and need arrivals to fetch it, and a telling in which the ground is theirs and the work is a repair suits that.

### What the sand-country people tell

It reaches a player through `deserter` and through nobody else.

It is right that a spent stone is carrion and a held one draws, and that every store draws. It is right that the first comers' country was full of packs. It is right that the stones are what brought the great animals to where they lie, though it reaches that from a belief that is false: that wards are what bring feeders in the first place. Feeders come up in any unlit place, ward or no ward.

It is wrong that its own forebears were born of the sand and owed the builders nothing. They descend from the same arrivals. It is wrong that walking out saved anything: no village was saved by it. It credits the easy nights of the unhunted generations to its people's burning of what they killed, as householders credit them to the forebears. Both are wrong about the same thing. The nights were easy because the hosts were whole, and no telling on the ground says so.

### The ground and the animals

They show the order of hardness, lees set into crystal in rock nobody has opened, salt failed in the old stores and nests where it failed, moss on the surface stone, and the size of the great feeders. They show where each great feeder lies, which is where its own kind were boiled down, and that each still comes to bait of its own kind.

They give an order and no spans. Bulk says that an animal has been long unhunted and does not say how long.

### Arrivals, and a crew's own telling

Arrivals know the frame. They know wards, lees, feeders, dead wood and thinning worlds from home. They know nothing of this ground, and they take their words for it from householders along with the Guide, altar and offering included.

A crew also has a telling of its own: who came up first, which stone was taken first, what the nights were like before. It is the only history in the game that has witnesses, and it has one standing error. A person who comes up late takes the nights as found for the nature of the ground. An arrival who comes up after three first killings has never seen a dark night with nothing on the ground, and has no reason to think there ever was one.

### What no source supplies

Three parts of the firm history are in nobody's mouth and in no stone: that the way failed at its far end, that the builders were arrivals, and that the hosts were whole through the quiet generations. A player can reach the second and third from evidence. Nothing on the ground gives the first.

Two questions are open for the people building the game as well as for anyone on the ground. Later text may add evidence on either side. It may not settle them without changing this chapter.

1. **When the sand-country forebears left.** If they left while stones were held, they refused a working country. If they left after the dark, they left one that had already failed. The two peoples each need one of these to be true.
2. **The road outward.** Whether any pathfinder went down the dead road after the junction failed, and whether one who did came out anywhere. Arrivals want the answer to be yes. The born have no telling in which anyone left.

## 2.9 Unknown, and to whom

- How a living world came to lie on a dead root. Unknown to everyone on the ground, and not settled here.
- Whether the world that grew on the root had peoples. Unknown to everyone.
- What hollowed the root. Unknown to everyone, and left so.
- Where this ground hangs on the Tree. Known to the first pathfinders, and gone with the last of them.
- Whether the tear is one or several, and how long it will stay open. Unknown to arrivals. The born do not know there is a tear.
- How long ago anything was. Unknown inside the world. Firm only as the ranges in 2.1.

## 2.10 What the present owes to each time

**Before people.** The wood. Without it there is no floor, no set ground, no bed that brings a person back. The islands, their metals, and somewhere for a second gate to open. The order of hardness, and so the ten tiers. Crystal. Whole hosts with a strongest over each, which is the state of the ground on the first night of play.

**The first comers.** Every structure a player finds. The great stones, and with them the fact that each great feeder lies at a known place: it goes to stones that smell of its own kind, and those stones are there because the first comers boiled its kind down on them. The baits lying ready. The trades of the townsfolk and the Guide. The born themselves. The division of labour, which the born take for the natural order and which was made in households of this time.

**The failing of the way.** The fewness of everyone. The lack of any pathfinder, and so the lack of any way to leave. A rule that waits for the late game: a country held by great charges lasts only while people go on coming up at the mouth.

**The end of the hunting.** No born person goes to a great stone, and all eight townsfolk still act on that. The full stores under the rock. The broken stores on the surface. The sand-country people as a separate people.

**The unhunted generations.** The size of the great feeders. The easy first nights. The nests. The empty villages. The born's need for lees, which is why every householder buys anything. The words altar and offering. The belief that peopls were never born anywhere, which could only arise among people who had not seen an arrival in any forebear's time. And the fact that every first killing takes from the born a protection they credit to their forebears.

**The present.** The nights as the crew has made them. A late arrival comes up into those nights and has no other measure.
