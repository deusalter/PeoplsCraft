# 5. The peoples

This chapter says who lives on the ground, what each people holds above itself, who rules it and why, what it believes and what follows from that in play. It then takes the eight townsfolk one at a time, says what the peoples make of the players, and says where a crew's roles come from.

Things are called by plain description or by their keys. Section numbers beginning with 1 point to the ground chapter. Lines of speech given here avoid every game thing that is still waiting for a name.

## 5.1 One stock and one difference

Everyone on the ground is of one stock. Householders, the sand-country people and arrivals have the same bodies, the same span of life and the same needs. Every line on the ground began with someone who came up at the mouth of the dead road (chapter 2).

One bodily fact divides them. A person who walked up through the root has a way open in the dead wood, and the way takes that person when the body fails. A person born on the ground has no way and dies where killed. A parent does not pass the way on. The walk up makes it and nothing else does.

That fact cuts both ways. It lets an arrival hunt, and it bars an arrival from the hearthstone (1.6). So each kind of person does one half of what a held country needs, and neither can do the other's half.

The two born peoples are divided by where their forebears lived when the hunting stopped. One had wards that worked and went on boiling down what it killed. The other lived on sand, where a ward had never turned the kind that mattered, and gave up the pot.

No other people lives on the ground, in the hollow or on the islands (5.4).

## 5.2 Householders

### What a householder is now

A householder is a born person trained to hold a house ward: a pot of lees, and a hand on a hearthstone until first light. The word is older than their present condition. Few of them have a house.

A fixed hearth uses lees every night. A born person can get lees in two ways. One is to kill small feeders by day or at the edge of a hearth's reach, which fills a pot thinly and sometimes kills the hunter. The other is to open an old store. The stores that could be reached from the surface were opened generations ago. The sealed ones lie under solid rock in the dark, with nests in them, and a born person who digs down to one does not come up.

So most householders carry the pot and the stone: a clay pot of lees, and a slab of hard stone with the lines cut in it. They walk by day and sit down at dusk in whichever old house is in reach, with a hand on the slab. A hearth, as they use the word, is any place where one of them is holding tonight. The game does not show them on the road (3.7).

A householder with a fixed hearth is a householder with a supply. At present the only supply is an arrival who hunts. A `guest_bed` in a sound room is that supply offered. This is why each of them comes to a stranger's roof and stays, and why each leaves the morning the bed is broken.

### What they hold above themselves

A stone held by one hand from dusk to first light, and held again the next night.

A householder is judged by other householders on what can be seen: the hand, which long holding marks, and who has learned at their stone. The largest form of the same thing is the country as their telling has it, with every great stone lit. In their mouths *lit* always means held. A stone with a hand on it at night has a lamp beside it, and a dark stone is one nobody is holding. *First light* is the hour a holder lifts the hand. Game text about light at the great stones should carry these meanings and no other.

Holding stands above the holder. It takes the arm first. The arm shakes at first light and the palm is raw, and in an old holder the hand has lost its feeling and most of its strength.

### Who rules, and why nobody does

The cause is in the ward. Holding is one person's work, and others standing by add nothing to it (rule 7). From dusk to first light every holder is fixed to a stone. No householder can go to another's hearth at the only hour that matters. None can make another safer, so none can make another obey.

By day they walk and talk. What they have in common is carried that way: who is holding where, which hearths went dark, and the Guide. Nobody enforces any of it. A householder wronged by another has one answer, which is to sit down somewhere else and say why at the next hearth.

Holding is learned at a stone, at night, beside someone who is doing it. A holder has to feel where the edge lies, and that is learned by the hand. A householder who dies before anyone has learned at their stone leaves a hearth with nobody to sit at it. Some of the villages emptied that way, beside those that emptied for want of lees (2.5).

What stands where rule would stand is lees. A pot of better lees turns more (rule 1). The best lees comes from the great stones, and only peopls go to the great stones.

### What they believe

**About the ground.** There is one ground and this is it. It has always been sound. It is theirs by descent: their forebears raised the stones and lit every one, and built to the ends of the ground. They have never needed a ward core, so they have no idea of one, and a pot and a hand are a whole ward to them. When an arrival says that other grounds are thinning, a householder does not believe there are other grounds.

**About the great feeders.** Each great one can be seen to hold a country. Lesser things stay off its ground and feed where it allows. A lit stone did the same work. So householders say the forebears set each animal over its country when the stones were raised, and that it has stood over it since. This is the belief behind the words on the boss bars.

In the quiet generations some of them acted on it. They laid small kills at the dark stones to keep the animal lying there, and it worked: carrion at a spent stone is what brings the strongest of a host and holds it (2.5). The stone on which something is laid for the one set over the country is an altar, and what is laid is an offering. The words are theirs, and the practice goes on among the householders who never come to a crew. The baits of the Guide are called offerings by the same word, though they are laid to bring the animal up and kill it.

**About peopls.** A person is someone born on the ground who dies when killed. A peopl looks like a person, arrives alone at the mouth with nothing, belongs to no hearth, and gets up after a killing. Most householders hold that the ground puts peopls out at the mouth and that they were never born anywhere. The reasoning is from the hearth. Everything a householder values is paid for with an arm and, in the end, a life, and a peopl cannot be made to pay either.

Beds and stones that only a peopl can use lie in every old house. Householders who think about it guess that the forebears had peopls working for them too. No telling says so.

**About the dead.** Death is final, and no householder says otherwise. Anything dead on land is food by night: the shade-kind comes to it (7.3). Nothing comes up in water. So a dead householder is carried to open water and sunk with a stone, and until the body can be carried it lies in a lit room with someone awake beside it. What a householder leaves is a stone and whoever learned at it.

### What follows

**Trade.** They take lees for anything and sell anything for lees. They buy whatever is offered, cheaply, because it can be carried on to the next hearth. Every exchange is settled on the spot and by weight. Nothing is lent and nothing is owed, since a peopl has no hearth that would answer for a debt.

**Teaching.** They teach the Guide freely, because every step of it ends with lees in a pot. They teach how to make each bait. They do not put a peopl's hand on a hearthstone, for the cause in 1.6: a peopl taken at the stone leaves a charge that nobody is driving.

**Refusing.** No householder sits through a night over the near end of a peopl's way. None will go to a great stone or through a gate. None takes a fired token in payment, though any of them will buy one for a little lees, as they buy anything.

**Fighting.** They do not fight. They hold or they leave. When packs come to the walls a householder is at the stone, and the fighting at the edge is done by someone else.

### Where they disagree

The split can be seen in who comes to a crew's rooms and who never does.

- Most hold that the stones should be lit again and that peopls are the means. The seven householders among the townsfolk are of this mind, or near it.
- Some hold that a hearth is enough. They say the great ones hold the dark stones well, that the nights were quieter before peopls began killing them, and that this is what peopls craft means. These go on laying offerings and do not come.
- A few say that a peopl is a person who came from somewhere.
- Many do not care what a peopl is, provided the pot is full by dusk.

### How they treat a peopl, day to day

- They call every arrival *peopl* to the face and do not ask for another name.
- They deal at once, by weight, and owe nothing afterwards.
- They tell a peopl what to do next, where, and what it opens. They do not say what it costs. This is the Guide, and it is the manner of all their talk.
- They take no notice of a fall. A peopl who goes down at the stones is expected back by morning.
- They do notice a householder's death. A hearth gone dark is told at every hearth on the road.
- They judge peopls crew by crew, by what comes into the pot. Lees has no maker's name on it.
- They will live under a peopl's roof. The hearth under that roof is the householder's.
- They count a child born to a peopl as a person (5.4).

## 5.3 The sand-country people

### What they are

They are born people whose forebears lived on the sand. The burrowing kind passes deep under the ground and crosses any ward (rule 6). In sand country a pot and a hand never turned the kind that came from below. They only drew (rules 4 and 5). When the great stones went dark, the people of the sand were the ones for whom a ward was all cost. They gave up the pot and burned what they killed, so that nothing they had would draw a feeder (2.4).

Then nobody hunted the burrower, and it let nothing else come up on its sand. For the whole of the quiet generations the sand country was the one country where a person could sleep without a ward, a pot or a watch. The burrower took some of them, and they held that a lighter price than a pot.

They lived by what the edge of the sand gives: flocks on the dry grass, the small runner of the sand, fired clay and glass. Fire was their craft before it was their defence. A kill burns clean only in a hard fire, and they have always had one standing.

With the burrower dead the sand is open to every pack. They hold no wards. What they have against the dark is light: feeders do not come up in a lit place (1.6). Their houses now stand inside ground that is lit all night by one fire, with people awake at its edge to deal with whatever walks in from the dark (3.2, `desert`). A fire of that size wants fuel, and the sand country grows very little that burns.

### What they hold above themselves

Ground with nothing on it that a feeder would come for.

While the burrower lived they also held the burrower above themselves, as householders hold a lit stone. It was the thing that held their country, and what it took was its due. The players killed it. That is the first thing the sand-country people know about any crew.

What is left above them is the fire. A kill given whole to it, with nothing held back, is the best thing a person among them can do. A kill boiled down and put in a pot is the worst.

They know their hard case. The lees they take from a broken store would hold their own houses through many nights. They burn it. Since the burrower died the dark has been taking their people at the edge of the firelight, and they still burn it.

### Who rules, and why

While the burrower lived, nobody ruled them. A great feeder's ground does what a ruler is for. It left no night to hold, no store to guard and no hunt to order. Those who quarrelled walked apart, since all the sand was equally safe.

Now rule lies with whoever holds a fire. The light that guards the houses and the heat that burns a kill clean are the same fire, and its fuel has to be carried in from wooded country and coal country. Whoever has the fuel decides who sleeps inside the light. No fire answers to another, which is why marches come unevenly and from no one direction.

### The fired token

Under a fire that has burned kills for generations, the sand runs to glass with the ash in it. A token (`ash_token`) is a piece broken from that floor. It is grey and smells of nothing, and it cannot be made any other way.

Whoever holds a fire gives tokens to those who march for it. A person who shows one at any fire is let inside the light for the night and fed, since every fire wants marchers. That is all a token is good for. A fighter on a march carries tokens because the walk home may be longer than the day. When a march is beaten the tokens lie where the fighters fell or ran, beside the lees they had taken.

A token charges no pot.

### What they believe

**About the ground.** The dark gives up feeders where kills are stored. A country with no stores would have none. Wards are what bring them. In their own country that was the whole truth for as long as the burrower lived. Elsewhere it is half of it: feeders come up in any unlit place, stores or none.

**About the stones.** The stones fattened the great ones. Every spent charge is carrion, and the builders filled their own country with packs until nobody could hold it.

**About the great feeders.** A great one holds a country better than any stone does. It lets nothing else in, needs no lees, and takes only what it takes.

**About peopls.** A peopl comes up out of the ground with nothing and goes to the old stonework. It eats what is stored there and nests beside it. It kills feeders and stores them. Kill it, and nobody sees it go, and another is there in the morning (1.6). The sand-country people see no difference worth a word between that and a host that gathers again at its stones. They class peopls with what a store draws.

**About the dead.** They burn their dead as they burn every kill, so that nothing of theirs is left to draw. Those the burrower took could not be burned, and they counted that its due. What a crew did with the burrower's body is the worst thing they know of (4.4).

### What follows

**Fighting.** There is no use in killing a thing that gets up. A march goes for what draws: chests, pots, forges of remains, gates, benches, and the bed a peopl comes back to. It marches so as to arrive at first light and goes home at dusk, because the only safe place it has is inside a fire's light. The lees it takes is carried home to be burned. In play the fighters go for the players and break nothing. Store-breaking is *proposed* (chapter 8).

**Trade.** They have nothing a householder wants and want nothing a householder has. What they are short of is fuel. A crew that mines coal and fells timber has the one thing every fire needs.

**Knowing.** They know how to burn a kill clean, how to live on lit ground through a night with no ward, and where every store in the sand country lies, since they have walked round those places all their lives. They tell none of it to a peopl now.

**Refusing.** Pots, salted lees, forges built round remains, gates, crystal-work, and above all a small feeder fed on unsalted remains. Crystal is lees to them and goes into the fire with the rest.

### Where they disagree

- The old at the fires say that another burrower will come onto the empty sand, and that the sand will close again if the sand stones are left alone. They are right about the animal (4.1). Every time a crew baits `altar2` it kills the one that had moved in.
- Those who march say there is no time for that, and that every store broken is a pack that does not come.
- Some have no place at any fire. They follow the dusk packs to the crews' houses, to reach a store while the pack has the crew busy, and carry what lees they find to a fire.
- A few have said what `deserter` says: that burning was right while the burrower lived and is wrong now. He is the only one who has acted on it.

### What a march cannot burn

A march that breaks a store finds more than lees in it: worked pieces of crystal and metal, salves, old gear, arrows with heads of hard metal. By the sand-country reckoning these have a kill in them, and they do not burn. What will not burn is not brought inside the light. It is left in the sand beyond the fire's edge.

Those with no place at a fire pick it over. `deserter` deals with them. They want tokens, which are a night inside the light. He wants the pieces, since a peopl will give tokens for them. Everything he sells came to him this way.

## 5.4 Nobody else

**The hollow and the islands.** No people lives in either. A charge does not outlast the night, and where the hour stands there is no first light, so nobody can hold there and live. No fire could be fed there for ever. An arrival's bed marks nothing there. The first comers went to both and came back, as their vaults show. Whatever stands upright in those places is a feeder.

**Arrivals.** They come from several worlds. They share a trade and a way of arriving and nothing else. They have no chief, no common belief and no claim on one another beyond the agreement to work the same stones. An arrival who came up long ago differs from one who came up yesterday only in being older, since the way does nothing against age.

**The child of an arrival.** A child born on the ground has no way in the wood, whoever the parents are, and cannot follow a parent who hunts. Such a child is left at a hearth, learns at the stone and is a householder. That is how the born came to be.

**A born person and the mouth.** The born keep off the ground over the mouth (3.2). Nobody on the ground knows of a born person who went down the road. Whether a born person who walked it down and back would have a way is unknown to everyone.

## 5.5 The eight townsfolk

Seven are householders. Each has a trade, which is what that householder does by day. The eighth is a sand-country man. Townsfolk speak plainly and want something done. None of them explains the past, and none knows the firm version of it.

Each entry ends with two or three lines of speech as a guide to manner. Five lines now in the game give a word or a gesture power, or are sayings, and go: `beastcaller`'s "Things follow you if you ask correctly" and "An effigy is a promise. Keep it."; `apothecary`'s "A salve now is worth ten after"; `runescribe`'s "Mana is only light that has not decided what to be yet"; `wayfinder`'s "I keep it current".

### `wayfinder`

A householder with the longest road of any, who carries word between hearths and has the fullest telling of the Guide. A walker's hearth is wherever a room is offered, so this one comes to any ready room. A crew that takes the stones in order is what the telling says should happen, and this householder means to see it from a held hearth.

The stock is what every walker carries: torches, arrows, planks, salves. The bedrolls and the old recall stones come out of the old houses and the opened stores, and no born person has a use for either.

`wayfinder` knows where the great stones of each kind lie, how each bait is made, and which night the moon goes dark. The road has also shown this householder that the nights are worse since crews began killing at the stones. The telling leaves that out, and `wayfinder` judges it a price worth paying and does not put the price to a peopl. What this one asks of a crew is work along the road: an old house stocked, a nest cleared from a `ruin`, word of whether the floor at the next kind of stone is whole.

- "The crates at the next old houses are empty, so fill them if you pass that way."
- "The moon is dark in three nights, and you should be under a roof or where you mean to fight."
- "They said every stone on this road was lit once."

### `provisioner`

A householder whose trade is carrying goods from hearth to hearth. Word that a crew has more lees than its hearths use travels the road, and this is the one who comes for it (1.6).

The stock is meat, fruit, hide, feather and fibre, bought from hearths that hunt ordinary animals by day, with coal and arrows. Once a crew has crossed to the islands the stock includes their fruit, bought from crews as they come back (6.9).

`provisioner` knows what every hearth on the road is short of, how much a pot takes for a night, and that two held hearths close together bring packs at dusk. This householder takes the worsening nights for bad luck that enough lees will cover, and has never asked what a peopl is. What this one asks is supply: hide and meat from a named country, coal for the cold country.

- "I buy anything, because somebody two hearths on will want it."
- "Take food with you when you go under."
- "Two pots this close bring them at dusk, and I told the others so before I sat down here."

### `smith`

A householder whose trade is metal. She works at a fire by day and holds by night, and her hand shows both. An `anvil` means someone is working iron who will go deeper for harder ore, and she comes to it.

She can shape a bar of any metal. From `cinder` upward she cannot make one, because what loosens that ore is great remains in a ring of stone, and no born person can get great remains. She sells iron, gold, coal, lanterns and arrowheads of her own make. She sells each harder metal once crews have it to spare: `cinder` once the first forge of remains is burning, and `umbrite` once the strongest of the hollow has been killed, since that carcass is full of it.

She knows the order of hardness by trial, and that a forge left burning is a store with no hand on it. She has seen the metals of the hollow only as bars a crew brought her, and has never seen their ore. She has looked for a first comers' forge all her life and never found a ring standing (2.3). What she asks for is what she cannot go and look at: raw ore of a metal she has only seen poured, or a tool brought whole out of a sealed `dungeon`.

- "Iron lifts with a stone pick, and the pale gem under it does not."
- "I can work a bar of that metal and I cannot make one."
- "A forge of remains against my wall draws, and I am the one sitting up beside it."

### `apothecary`

A householder whose trade is the holder's body: the arm after a night at the stone, and the hand after a lifetime of nights. This one comes for the fresh remains of `boss:1`, the weakest great remains there are, which loosen only made things. The belief is that an old holder's dead hand has set, as metal sets, and that something which loosens made things might loosen it. Nobody knows whether that is so, and it has not worked yet. A crew that kills great feeders is also hurt often and pays at once.

The stock is salves for wounds, the tonic a holder drinks at first light, which does the same for an arm spent on a staff, and draughts for the skin and the legs. The stronger salve and tonic need growth from the hollow, and the broth needs its caps, so they come once a crew can cross (6.9). What this one asks for is remains brought fresh, caps from the hollow, and old salve out of a sealed store.

- "Put it on the table, away from the pot."
- "This is what a holder drinks at first light, and it does the same for your arm."
- "A second salve too soon does nothing, so wait."

### `tinker`

A householder whose trade is small worked pieces: knowing what each does, mending them, and joining two into one. Joining needs the remains of `boss:1` set into a bench, and no born person has had those in any living memory. This householder learned the trade from someone who had never done the joining either. The crew's bench is the first one they have seen, and it is why they came.

The stock is pieces gathered over a lifetime from opened stores and from other walkers. One piece has a part of `cinder` in it and comes into stock when `smith` has that metal. `tinker` knows what each piece does by holding it, and which store a piece came from by its make. This one believes the old pieces were made by a skill of the hands that has since been lost, when what stopped was the hunting that supplied the bench. What this one asks for is the small worked pieces from a sealed `dungeon`, which a crew after bars would leave behind.

- "I have turned these pieces over since I was small and never joined two until I saw your bench."
- "A second of the same adds nothing."
- "If you get into a sealed store, bring out the small things as well as the bars."

### `runescribe`

A householder whose trade is cutting lines in stone. The lines on a hearthstone carry what the hand drives down to the charge, and every hearth needs them cut true. A `runeloom` means someone is driving crystal through cut lines, which is the same work pointed outward. It also means a supply of crystal from deep rock, where no born person goes.

The stock is crystal, fibre laid round crystal, small pieces cut to help a driving arm, a plain staff, and new recall stones copied cut for cut from old ones. `runescribe` knows that a bad cut leaks into the arm, and that where to cut is learned by the hand and cannot be told. This one holds that crystal grows in dark rock and has no part of any kill in it. This one has also held a recall stone all night to see what it would do, and concluded that the stone works on what a peopl is, having no idea of a way in the wood. What this one asks about is the great stones, which no born hand has cut and no householder will visit: how many lines leave the place for the hand.

- "A staff is the same work as a hearthstone, pointed away from you."
- "A bad cut leaks into the arm."
- "I held one of those stones all night once and it did nothing for me."

### `beastcaller`

A householder who feeds small feeders on unsalted remains and lets them fight at the edge. A holder cannot fight, since the hand is on the stone, and a lone householder has nobody to stand at the edge. This is one answer to that. The other householders dislike it, because unsalted remains are food and food draws.

The trade lives on the remains of the shade-kind, which go into every carved bone (6.6). A born person can come by them only in small amounts. A crew that has killed the strongest of that kind can supply them, and since that kill the shade-kind runs in packs and its remains are to be had at every dusk. That is why this householder comes then.

The stock is bone, carved bone with remains bound on, raw remains of the commonest kinds, and a whistle that lets a hand hold a second small one. `beastcaller` knows that the small one stays for the food and for nothing else. This one thinks the whistle works by its sound, when the work is done by what is packed in it. What this one asks about is nests and the dark night: what kind was breeding in a `brood_nest`, told before the nest is broken.

- "It stays for what is on the bone."
- "Do not bring it inside my walls, because unsalted is food and I sit beside a pot."
- "If you find a nest in the old stonework, tell me what kind was in it before you break it."

### `deserter`

A sand-country man who came with a march and did not go home with it. The march was beaten. He could not make the walk to a fire before dark, and the only lit, walled place in reach had a pot in it. He went in. A man who has passed a night under a ward has no place inside any fire's light.

He holds his own hearth. Nobody put his hand on a stone beside theirs. He had watched it done, through walls he was sent to break, and he does it badly and at more cost to the arm than a taught holder pays. His pot was first filled from lees his own march had carried out of the crew's chests. Since then he buys cheaply for lees and sells on to walkers, as the others do.

He sells for tokens only: worked pieces, strong salves and hard-headed arrows that marches took from stores and could not burn (5.3). He knows when a march will come and what it will go for, which stores in the sand country have been broken, and what the old at the fires say about the sand stones. He believes the burrower's ground was a safety his people chose and could choose again. What he asks of a crew is what he can no longer do for his own people: empty a sealed store in the sand country before a march reaches it, so that nobody dies breaking it; leave `altar2` unbaited through a round of the moon; carry fuel to within sight of a fire and leave it.

- "I marched with them and I will not again."
- "A token is a night inside the light at any fire of ours."
- "While the burrower lived nothing else came up on the sand."

*Proposed:* one further line, said once, on what was in the burrower's body when the crew boiled it down (4.4).

## 5.6 The players

### What they are called

Householders say *peopl* of one and *peopls* of many, to the face and behind the back. It is their word for persons with a sound left out (1.5). It carries no insult. It is the plain name of a kind, as *feeder* is.

The sand-country people speak the same tongue and use the same word. On their side it has only its second sense: the kind that did this to the country.

Arrivals have taken the word for themselves. Among themselves they also say *crew* and *cutter*, which are words of the trade they came with.

Householders count `deserter` as a person. He was born on the ground and can be lost. They dislike him.

### What each people notices about a crew

Both born peoples judge peopls by the crew and by the house, so whatever a crew has earned with them is held in common by everyone who works the same stones. A late arrival comes into what the crew already has. Word of a crew travels the road among householders, and from fire to fire with the fuel. None of this is in play. It is *proposed* as the ground for a shared standing, and chapter 8 sets it out as a table.

**Householders** notice lees and nights. A crew rises with them by lees weighed into a pot, which is every purchase; by the first kill of each kind, since that is a better charge than any pot has had; by each householder housed and each night that hearth stays held; by packs beaten off at the walls; by old houses on the road stocked and cleared of nests. It falls with them by a bed broken under a householder, by a hearth overrun while its holder sat at it, by great remains left unheld against a householder's wall, by lees burned, and by any dealing at a fire.

What that opens, in order: more of what each householder has to sell, and a better weight for what a crew sells them. Tasks. What householders have seen each killing let loose, which the Guide leaves out. Last and hardest, a householder who will go to a great stone and sit at it. No householder has done that in any telling, and it is what a crew needs before a great stone can be held (4.10).

**The sand-country people** notice what a crew burns and what it leaves alone. A crew rises with them by a kill burned and not boiled down, which pays no coin; by lees from an old store burned; by nests broken; by a forge of remains pulled down or a gate shut; by fuel carried to a fire; by `altar2` left unbaited. It falls with them by every bait laid at `altar2`, by every march broken, by every householder housed, and by a great stone held.

What that opens, in order: marches that pass a crew's houses by. Trade at a fire in tokens, without `deserter` between. What they know of the sealed stores of the sand country. Fighters who will walk with a crew by day against a nest or a store. Their telling of the stones, which no householder will give.

Lees goes into a pot or into a fire. So most things a crew does with a kill raise it with one people and lower it with the other.

## 5.7 Where a crew's roles come from

Nobody on the ground is born to a role and nothing gives one. Every arrival grew up beside wards and has watched this work done. A person gets better at what they practise, and anyone can take up any of it. Each is the work of one body, with a material, at a cost. None is made stronger by others doing it alongside (rule 7).

A held ward draws feeders as far as its edge, and the holder cannot fight. So wherever a ward is held, someone stands between the animals and the hand on the stone. The game's melee style is that work, done in front of a great feeder so that the rest can do theirs.

Arrows cross a ward freely. The bow is how a held place kills, from inside, what it has turned back and what flies over. The game's ranged style is that work.

A holder drives a charge through lines cut in stone and pays in the arm. A staff is crystal on cut lines, and driving it is the same act pointed at a body, at the same cost. The game's magic style and the blue bar are that work.

Anyone who boils down kills knows that unsalted remains are food. A totem uses that knowledge the other way: it carries food, and a small feeder forms on it and stays (1.6). The game's summoning style is that work.

Digging, building and smelting are the crews' own trade. A cutter parts set matter along its joints with an edge hard enough for the piece, in a place that wants the cutter dead. The root cannot be cut, so the skill goes into ordinary ground.

One trade is missing. A crew goes into dead wood behind a pathfinder. These crews lost theirs, and there is none on the ground. For that reason nobody can leave.
