# 1. The ground

This chapter says what the world of the game is, where it stands among other worlds, and why each thing a player meets works as it does. Later chapters name things and fill in the peoples, the creatures, the eight great feeders and the history. Until the naming pass, game things are called by plain description or by their internal keys.

## 1.1 The world in one page

The game's ground is a small world lying on a dead root.

The root belonged to an earlier world. Nothing made on this ground cuts it. Wood of this kind does one thing: it holds the ground over it steady. On other worlds a piece the size of a fist costs more than the town it guards. Here it is the floor of everything.

Three things follow from the root, and most of the game follows from them.

Ground here stays as it is put. A stone set in the air stays in the air.

The wood turns no animal. The things that eat at night come up in the dark all over this ground. Against them a household needs only the cheap part of a ward: a pot of the boiled-down remains of those same animals, and one trained person with a hand on the hearthstone until morning.

People who came up through the root are still joined to it. The way each of them came by stays open in the dead wood. A body beaten to the end of its strength drops into that way and comes out where it last made its bed. People born on this ground have no such way. They die where they are killed.

So the dangerous work here falls to outsiders. Those who came up through the root hunt. Those born here hold the hearths. The born have a word for the outsiders which is close to their word for persons and is a different word. It is rendered *peopls*. The outsiders' trade is to kill feeders and boil them down, and above all to bait the strongest animal of a host onto old stone, kill it and render it. That trade is peopls craft. Every hearth on this ground is held with what it yields. The nights also worsen by it, because a host whose strongest is killed and left uneaten breaks into packs.

The players are peopls.

## 1.2 The frame this ground stands in

These are facts of the wider cosmos, true on every world. The game does not alter them.

**Worlds.** The Ashvattha, the Tree, bears the worlds. A world is a whole reality with its own ground, sun, moon and stars. Nothing lies beyond its sky and there is no space between one world and another. A world is never a planet. One world is reached from another along the Tree's wood, by roads that run in its roots.

**The speech and its failing.** Every world is held in being by a continuing speech, the Adi-Vak. In the present age that speech is failing. The failing is called the Fraying. Ground it reaches turns unsound: a measured distance comes out different each time, a track is there one day and absent the next. Then the ground thins, and then it is gone. Nobody on this ground knows why the speech is failing, and no text of the game gives a reason.

**The wood.** The Tree is no part of the speech and does not fail with it. Worlds ended before the present ones, and their roots remain in the Tree as dead wood. Dead root-wood is black and cold and heavier than stone. Fire and rot do nothing to it. It holds the ground over it steady against the Fraying.

**Wards.** People made wards. No god gave them, and they are made on every world. A ward is four things.

- A housing of hard stone, carved, with a worn place for the hand.
- A core of dead root-wood, which steadies the ground.
- A charge of lees: the dark residue left when killed feeders are boiled down, packed in salt. A driven charge tells feeders that the ground belongs to a killer of feeders.
- A holder: one trained person with a hand on the stone, who drives the charge and pays for it in the body. The arm shakes and the hand loses its colour.

The rules of a ward are fixed, and the game uses them as they stand.

1. A ward turns away any feeder weaker than the strongest kill boiled into its charge.
2. A charge does not outlast the night.
3. Wood with no charge steadies the ground and turns nothing.
4. A charge that nobody is driving, or a spent one, is a carcass to feeders. It draws them.
5. A held ward also draws feeders, as far as its edge, and that is where the fighting is.
6. People, weapons, arrows and fire cross a ward freely. So do feeders that stay in the air and feeders that pass deep under the ground.
7. A hold is one person's work. Others standing with the holder add nothing to it. No word, song, gesture or gathering has any effect, on this ground or anywhere.

**Feeders.** The things wards were made against are the Rel'shari. The plain word is feeders, and the game's text uses the plain word. Their low ranks have no self and no speech. The lowest, Kṣudhita, have no lasting shape and form round whatever they are feeding on. Those that have fed long enough to hold a body, Jāgrat, run in hosts. A feeder eats more than flesh. It eats the hardness out of timber and the set out of a wall. A trained fighter can kill a low feeder. Feeders with a self exist elsewhere. None is on this ground and none is to be written here.

Three rules of feeders carry much of the game.

1. A host goes where its strongest goes.
2. Among feeders a kill is eaten. One that kills and eats the strongest is followed in its place. When the strongest is killed by people, who do not eat it, nothing is followed and the host breaks into packs.
3. A weaker feeder stays off a stronger one's ground and eats there only on sufferance. An old feeder goes back to places where its own kind have died.

## 1.3 This ground

**Its place.** It is a small world. No living road reaches it. One dead road does, through the root it lies on. Where it hangs on the Tree is unknown to everyone on it. Arrivals say their own worlds are thinning, and that this is the one place they have stood where the ground was sound in every direction.

**The root.** The block the game calls `bedrock` is the root's upper face. Cores on other worlds are cut from wood that is already broken or has come to the surface. This root is whole, and no edge made here marks it. Wood that holds ground wears. Whether this root is wearing is not known to anyone.

A ward with wood and no charge has sound ground and no defence. That is the condition of this whole world, by rule 3.

**Three places in one hold.** The root holds three places.

- The living ground (dimension 0). A whole small world with its own sky.
- The hollow (`dim:1`). The inside of the root, with the wood for floor and for roof, and rock, standing water and fungus between. It has no sky.
- The islands (`dim:2`). What is left of the world that grew on this root while the root lived. Its ground is gone except for islands in two layers. Its sky stopped at one dusk.

The hollow and the islands are remains of an earlier world, of the kind the wider cosmos calls Pūrvaśeṣa. In such remains the order of things is damaged. The hour of the day does not move. A fall off the last ground does not land: the faller is back on the last footing, hurt.

**The sky of the living ground.** A sun. A moon that rises as the sun sets and sets as it rises. Stars that turn. A dark edge crosses the moon's face in a round of eight nights: the moon is whole on the first night, dark on the fifth, and still a quarter shadowed on the eighth. There are no seasons and no year. The round of eight nights is the only calendar. Sun, moon and stars cannot be reached. What darkens the moon is a fact of this sky, like the length of the day, and is given no cause.

**Walking on.** Nobody has come to an edge or crossed their own track. Fourteen kinds of country recur, and the old great stones recur with them, each kind at its own spacing. No text or art may show an end of the ground.

**Down and up.** Digging ends at the wood. A body that somehow gets beneath the floor comes in at the top of the sky and falls from there, because a world has no outside. Building upward reaches nothing.

**Leaving.** The two gates lead to the hollow and the islands, which are parts of the same root. The one way off is the dead road, whose mouth is the place where every arrival first stood (the game's world spawn). The road needs a pathfinder and there is none on this ground. An arrival who left would lose the join to the root described in 1.6. Play follows nobody out.

## 1.4 Who is here

The chapter on peoples treats these in full.

**Arrivals.** On every world, crews go into dead roots behind a pathfinder to cut cores. This root is the largest sound piece of dead wood any crew has found, and it cannot be cut. Crews that lose their pathfinder in it wander until they come up at its one mouth. What lives in a dead road lets a lone walker pass and takes a company, so they come up one at a time. They lose their gear below. The order of things is worse damaged in the road than anywhere a gate reaches, and crewmates who went in during the same week can come up seasons apart. The players are these people.

**Householders.** People born here. They are few. They hold hearths, each with a trade, and walk by day between the old houses. No householder rules anything beyond a hearth. The game's townsfolk are householders, except `deserter`.

**The sand-country people.** People born here who do not render. They burn every kill to ash, so that nothing they store draws a feeder. Until `boss:2` was killed they lived on its ground. They are the people of the `ashen` event.

## 1.5 What Peopls Craft is

The born have a word for themselves and another for those who come up at the mouth of the root. The second is rendered *peopls*. In the speech of this ground the two words are close, and the rendering holds them close. One of them is a peopl.

The born use the word of anyone who arrives alone, owns nothing, belongs to no household, and gets up again after a killing. It means one who looks like a person and does not die when killed. Many of the born hold that peopls are put out by the ground at the mouth and were never born anywhere. Arrivals have taken the word for themselves.

*Craft* is the work the born cannot do and live: hunting feeders for their remains, baiting a host's strongest onto old stone, killing it and boiling it down. It covers everything that has a kill in it: lees, charges, forges, gates, crystal-work.

Peopls Craft is the name of the players and of what they do.

The word does work in play in three ways.

- **The division of labour.** The born hold and peopls hunt. All eight townsfolk wait for an arrival to supply them. None of them goes to a great stone.
- **No ruler.** Peopls came singly and cannot be killed into obedience. Nobody born commands them and they have no chief of their own. A crew is people who agreed to work the same stones.
- **Two uses.** Householders say peopls craft of the trade they live by. They also say it of the dark stones and the bad nights, meaning that outsiders did it. The sand-country people use it only the second way.

One fact under the word is unknown to the born. Their own forebears came up at the same mouth. The child of an arrival is born on the ground, has no way in the wood, and dies like anyone. Every householder descends from peopls. The old stores still hold bedrolls and stones cut to open a way that no born person has.

In play today the words appear only on the title screen. The text pass should put *peopls* in the mouths of townsfolk, as their ordinary word for the player.

## 1.6 The mechanics, in the order a player meets them

### Coming up with nothing

A new player stands at the mouth of the dead road with empty hands. Other players stand at the same mouth because the road has one. A player who joins a world months old is a crewmate or a later crew, come up late for the reason given in 1.4. They find the ground built on and the nights as bad as earlier kills have made them, because the hosts are common to the whole ground.

### Breaking ground

Ground that lies in the root's hold is set. It parts along even joints a stride apart and comes away in whole pieces. A piece put down stays where it is put, with or without anything under it. Cut the foot from a tree and the rest hangs. This is true in all three places. Living bodies, loose things, water and molten rock are not set. They fall and run as they would anywhere.

A tool lifts a piece whole only if the tool is harder than the piece. A softer tool still breaks the piece, slowly, into nothing worth carrying. The `Needs a ... pickaxe` prompt is that rule.

### Making

Making by hand, at a `workbench` and at an `anvil` is ordinary carpentry, masonry and smithing. No pattern is a spell.

### Food and ordinary animals

A body that cuts stone and fights gets hungry, heals when fed and wastes when starved. The animals that run from people are ordinary animals of this ground. They give meat and hide. They pay no coin, because they are no feeders.

### Night, light and feeders

Every hostile creature in the game is a low feeder, except the `ashen` fighters, who are people. Feeders come up out of the root. Nobody sees one arrive. They are in a dark place that was empty, and then in the next one, with no ground crossed between.

Lamps and waking people hold low feeders off, and they do not come up in lit places. That is why nothing hostile appears in block light. Caves are dark at all hours.

Low feeders are bodied in what they feed on. The woodland kinds are thorn and sprout, the sand kinds are grit, the kinds of the hollow are spore and fungus, the kinds of the islands are splintered crystal. One that nests in a store of iron is bodied in rust. What a feeder drops when it is killed is what it had been eating. That is why creatures drop wood, ore, bars and bone.

Two kinds, `gloomshade` and `moonspawn`, took their bodies where there is no sun, in the hollow and beneath it. Full sun undoes them. The low sun of dusk does not, so packs that set out at dusk arrive whole.

### Coin

Coin is lees, weighed out. Every hearth needs it every night, so every householder takes it for anything. Feeders yield it, and ordinary animals do not. The game pays at the kill and leaves out the night over the pot.

Salted and sealed, lees lasts. Old chests hold it.

### The bedroll, the fall and the stone

A crossing along the Tree's wood carries the same person through whole, with their wounds. In living wood a way closes behind the walker as the wood grows. Dead wood does not grow. A way made through it stays open behind the one who made it, and to that one only. Every arrival came up through this root, and the root lies under all three places. So an arrival is never off the wood in which their own way lies open.

The near end of a way is where its maker came out: at first, the mouth of the road. An arrival moves it by claiming a made bed on the living ground. That is the `bedroll`, and a hand laid to it at any hour is the claim. The way was made onto the living ground and comes out only there, so a `bedroll` in the hollow or on the islands holds nothing. If the `bedroll` is destroyed the near end goes back to the mouth.

A living body holds itself where it stands. A body at the end of its strength, from a blow, a burn, hunger or a drop, stops holding, and the way takes it. It comes out at the near end alive: hurt, hungry, with the arm half spent and the good of any draught gone. This is what the game shows as YOU FELL and Rise again. A fall in the hollow or on the islands ends at the same bed, because it is the same wood.

A body taken slack sheds what it carries loose. Lees is carried loose, by weight. About one part in seven is shed in the wood and eaten there. Gear is strapped on and comes through.

The `recall_stone` is the same crossing made on purpose. It is crystal and cut lines round a gem of the deep rock. Driven by the hand it loosens its bearer for a moment, as feeder-stuff loosens whatever it touches (see Great remains), and the way takes them. The bearer goes awake and sheds nothing. The stone is warm afterwards and will not work again until it cools.

The limits are part of the fact.

- The way takes a failing body. Nobody has seen it bring back one that was already dead.
- It does nothing against age or sickness.
- It belongs only to those who came up through the root, and only on ground that lies on the root.
- The born have no way. Feeders have none. They die where they are killed, which is why a creature killed stays killed.

Death is final on this ground, as on every world. Arrivals believe they cannot die here. They are wrong, and the game never shows them the case.

A `guest_bed` is a bed nobody has claimed. A born person will not lie down on the near end of a stranger's way.

### Sleep

A night slept is a night passed. Nobody sleeps with a feeder close by or with packs at the walls. With several players the night passes only when everyone on the living ground lies down.

### Fire, and the ten hardnesses

`forge1` is a wood or coal fire in a ring of stone. It melts the soft ores of the living ground, fires sand and clay, cooks meat and burns logs to coal.

Matter in the root's hold is set harder the nearer it lies to the wood and the longer it has lain there. On the living ground hardness rises with depth: `wood`, `stone`, `iron`, then `lumenite` and `cinder` in the deep rock. The hollow lies between two faces of the wood, and its metals, `umbrite` and `noctrium`, are harder again. The islands are the oldest matter in the hold, and theirs, `aether` and `prism`, are harder than those. Hardest is `eclipse`, in the lowest rock of the living ground, lying on the wood itself. The order in which each cuts the next was found by trial. Arrivals read it as an order of age, which is a guess.

Each hardness can be cut only with the one before. That is why a crew needs both gates before it can work its own floor.

From `cinder` upward no fire a person can light melts the ore. What does is given under Great remains.

### Why mine and make at all

A great feeder cannot be killed with wood. Each step in metal is taken to kill the strongest of the next host. The remains of that kill are what work the next metal. Householders gather to whoever is doing this, because great lees charges a hearth against everything weaker than the animal it came from.

### The Guide

The Guide is the order of work as householders tell it. It passes among arrivals by word of mouth, and `wayfinder` has the fullest telling. From the first room onward it is the order in which the born want the great stones taken, with how to make each bait. It says what each killing opens. It does not say what each killing lets loose.

### Rooms and townsfolk

Because the ground already lies on wood, a ward here needs no core. It needs a charge and a holder. A house ward on this ground is a pot of lees and a householder's hand on the hearthstone until first light.

A `guest_bed` in a room that is closed, has a door and a light, and is neither too small nor too large, is an arrival's offer to supply one hearth. The walls give the holder an edge to hold to. The lamp does part of the work inside. The door is there because a ward stops no person. One hand holds only so much ground, which is the limit on a room's size.

While the room is occupied, no feeder comes up within 28 strides of it. At night the householder stays within a step and a half of the bed, because the hand is on the stone. Break the bed and the householder walks on, and the ground round the house is open again. A player's own `bedroll` protects nothing: nobody is holding there.

Each householder comes when there is trade for them: `wayfinder` to any ready room; `provisioner` when an arrival carries forty coin, since lees in that amount can be smelled; `smith` to an `anvil`; `tinker` and `runescribe` to their benches; `apothecary` to the fresh remains of `boss:1`; `beastcaller` once the host of `boss:3` is broken and its small ones follow whoever feeds them; `deserter` after a march of his own people has been beaten off. They buy anything, cheaply, because anything can be carried on to another hearth.

No householder will settle in the hollow or on the islands. A charge does not outlast the night and a holder rests at first light. Where the hour stands there is no first light, and a hold that cannot end kills the holder.

Two held hearths are enough to draw packs (rule 5). A pack led by something stronger than the charge crosses it (rule 1), and people cross any ward (rule 6). So the protection round a home is gone for as long as `raid` or `ashen` lasts.

### Practice, the arm, crystal and totems

A person gets better at what they do. Levels and attribute points are the game's measure of that and nothing more.

The blue bar measures the arm. Driving anything through cut lines costs the body as holding a ward costs it. The bar shows how much more the arm will take before it stops answering. Nothing is stored in it and rest brings it back.

Crystal is old lees. A feeder that dies uneaten leaves its lees in the ground. The sun does this to the shade-kind at every dawn. Lees that lies long in the root's hold sets hard, as everything there does. That is the geode (`mana_ore`), and it is found in all three places. On the `runeloom`, crystal is bound to wood and fibre so that a hand can point it. A staff, driven, throws at a body what feeder-stuff does to anything set: it loosens it.

A totem is a small unsalted charge of one kind's remains. Unsalted lees is food. A low feeder of that kind forms on it and stays by the hand that carries its food, on sufferance, as any weaker feeder does beside a stronger. It goes for other feeders because other kinds are food to it. It is gone when the bearer goes down.

Trinkets are small worked pieces with crystal or remains in them. Each does one thing, and a second of the same adds nothing.

No two pieces of set metal work alike, and a thing taken from a feeder's body varies more, since the body was built of whatever the animal ate. That is the random prefix on gear, commoner on drops than on made things.

### The old stonework

Arrivals find the ground already built on. The history is in 1.7.

- `wardstone` is the dressed stone of the first comers.
- A `village` is houses round a well, whole and empty. Householders on the walk pass nights in them beside a lamp, which is why the lamps are lit and the crates stocked.
- A `dungeon` is a sealed store: rooms cut under solid rock with no way in from above, holding lees, gear and made baits. `cache1` and `cache2` are the same work in the hollow and on the islands.
- A `ruin` is a small surface store that has been broken into.
- A `brood_nest` is a store where the salt failed. Unsalted lees is food, feeders came to it, and they have bred there since. This is why nests are found in old stonework and nowhere else.

### The great stones, the bait and the great feeders

The eight blocks `altar1` to `altar8` are great ward housings, cut by the first comers to carry a charge made from the strongest of a host. Such a charge, held, turned that whole host from a wide country. Nothing made here breaks one. The paved floor, the pillars and the lamps round each are a killing floor.

Their charges were spent long ago. By rule 4 a spent stone is a carcass of the kind last boiled into it, and by the third rule of feeders an old one goes back to where its own kind have died. So the strongest animal of each host lies at the stones that smell of its own. It has fed there, unhunted, for longer than any householder's telling reaches. That is why the eight are far larger than anything else on the ground. Their size is bulk, and a well-armed crew can kill one.

What the game calls an offering is bait: raw remains of that host's lesser animals, in a vessel made to reach one beast. A burrower is reached by a lure sunk in sand. A flier that never lands is reached by a kite. Smoke carries in the still air of the hollow. A mask is worn by the one who means to be found. Shape, sound and gesture do nothing. The remains do the work. Four baits lie ready in old stores, and they still work, since a bait works by its material.

Laid on the stone, bait is a charge nobody is driving. The lesser animals leave it to the strongest, which comes up to eat. It eats the bait as it comes, so the bait is gone whether the crew wins or loses. While it is up, lesser feeders stay off its food and any `raid` or `ashen` attack breaks off. It stays while anyone who has fought it stands within 80 strides of the stone. With nobody left it goes back down. No second great feeder comes up into another's fight.

The next animal to answer bait at stones of that kind is a different one, grown into the room the first left. Every forge, gate and bench needs its own remains, so crews go on calling them.

`boss:7` answers only at night, because the sun undoes its kind.

### What a first killing does

Three things are lost each time a crew first kills the strongest of a host.

**The host.** Nobody ate it, so nothing is followed. The host breaks into packs over the whole ground. Packs with nothing over them are food for every other kind, and what eats grows. In play every hostile creature hits harder and takes more killing after each first kill, and the creatures that come in attacks more so. Later kills of the same kind change nothing more: that host is already broken. One player's first kill is every player's.

**Its ground.** While it lived, lesser things stayed off its ground and fed where it allowed. Play shows this for three of the eight.

- `boss:1`. Its packs go for stores at dusk. Great remains in a crew's chests are a store, and so are two householders' pots under neighbouring roofs. This is `raid`, and why it starts with either cause. It breaks at dawn.
- `boss:2`. Nothing lesser came onto the burrower's ground, and the sand-country people lived there on those terms. It took some of them and they held that a lighter price than rendering. With it dead their country is open to every pack the crews have loosed. They march on the crews' houses at first light, when the shade-kind are off the ground and the night's charges are spent, and go home at dusk, because they hold no wards. This is `ashen`. They take lees from every store they break, to burn it, and a beaten march leaves that lees behind. Their fighters are paid in fired tokens (`ash_token`), since a people that burns its kills has no lees to pay with. A token fills no pot, so householders refuse it. `deserter` takes them because he can still turn them into goods among his own people. Once `boss:2` is dead a few of them also come in behind the dusk packs.
- `boss:3`. See the next heading.

For the other five the game shows only the general hardening. The chapter on the eight says what each held off.

**What it was made of.** A feeder's body holds what it fed on, and an old one's holds things found nowhere else. Eaten by another feeder, that would have passed on. Rendered, it is lees, a furnace bed, a gate.

### The eighth night

Under the hollow, in the deepest part of the root, is a stock of feeders that rises once in every round of the moon, always on the eighth night. They are `moonspawn` and `lunar_howler`. They give a pale violet light, and when they are up in numbers the night sky takes that colour.

For as long as anyone knows, the rising never reached the surface. The strongest of the shade-kind, `boss:3`, held the ways up as its ground and fed on each rising as it came. With `boss:3` dead the rising comes all the way up, every eighth night, from the night after the kill. Other packs stay off the ground that night, so no `raid` falls on it. The sun undoes the risen at dawn. The pale slivers they carry (`moon_sliver`) are bits of their own bodies.

The timing is the animals' own. Nobody on the ground knows more, and no text should offer more.

### Great remains: forges, the bench, gates

A feeder eats the set out of things. The rendered remains of a great one go on doing that in small. On a ground where the wood sets everything hard, this is the one thing that loosens.

- **Forges.** `forge2` to `forge5` are each the remains of one great feeder in a ring of eight stones. Fire wakes the remains, and they take the set out of ore that no fire melts. A stronger animal's remains loosen harder ore. The ring bounds the working. A forge left burning goes on burning, so it works while its owner is away.
- **The bench.** The remains of `boss:1` are the weakest of the eight and loosen only made things. On the `tinker` bench two worked pieces are loosened far enough to be joined into one.
- **Gates.** A crossing needs a real relation to the far side, an opening, and the same person carried through. A gate kit is a frame of hard matter round the remains of an animal whose kind came from the far place: `boss:3`, whose kind came up out of the hollow, and `boss:5`, whose kind came down off the islands. The remains hold the relation and loosen the opening. Because the wood is under all of the living ground, a gate can be raised wherever its frame will stand. Pull a piece from the near frame and the remains come away and the opening shuts. The far mouth is only where the opening comes out. Nothing there is doing the work, so nothing there can be pulled down.

Stores draw. A forge is a store of great remains with no hand on it, and so is a chest of them.

### The hollow and the islands

Crews go there for the harder matter, for the great feeders at the first comers' stones, and for the sealed stores. Feeders are up at all hours in both, since neither has a sun. No pack or march comes there, because the stores are on the living ground.

### After the last

`altar8` is the first stone the first comers set. It lies under a mound of piled stone ringed with lamps. The key splits the capstone and the mound does not close again. What comes out is bodied in dressed stone. It has fed on housings. The kind of `boss:7`, leaderless by then, falls in behind it as the strongest thing present.

After its first killing no host on the ground has a strongest that people did not kill. The crew holds the strongest kill there is. A charge made from it would turn everything left, and a great stone could be held again with it. The game's present text calls a held stone lit, because a holder sits up beside a lamp. Nothing in play makes that charge yet. The later systems start here: a great stone a player can charge and hold, one night at a time, at the cost of going on killing.

## 1.7 The history, by source

Nobody on the ground has dates for any of this. Arrivals know nothing of its past.

**The stonework.** Great stones of eight kinds at even spacings, the weakest kind closest together and the strongest furthest apart, as reach would fall if each was held with a charge of its own host's strongest. Sealed stores under rock. Vaults in the hollow and on the islands, so the first comers had gates, so they had killed the kinds of `boss:3` and `boss:5`. Made baits for four of the eight. Bedrolls and recall stones. The stonework shows a numerous people who were joined to the root, hunted at the stones and held the whole country. It does not show how that ended.

**What householders say,** chiefly `wayfinder`. Their forebears raised the stones and every one was lit. The hunters were lost at the stones, the great lees ran out, and no pot filled with small kills could turn what walked by then. Kill the great ones again and the stones can be lit again. This telling makes the ground theirs, the emptying a misfortune and the arrivals hired hands. It leaves out what every kill lets loose. It leaves out that the forebears were what they now call peopls.

**What the sand-country people say,** as far as `deserter` tells it. The stones are what fattened the great ones. Every spent charge is carrion and every store draws. The first comers filled their own country with packs until nobody could hold it. Their own forebears walked out, burned their pots and went to live where a great one let nothing else in. This telling makes rendering the fault and burning the stores a duty. It leaves out that they lived as a great feeder's herd, and that walking away saved no village.

**Where the tellings collide.** Who the first comers were. Whether the hunters were lost or stopped. Whether the animal under the mound was ever killed, and whether the mound was closed over a thing that could not be killed or over the best stone on the ground.

**Firm, for the people building the game.** The first comers were arrivals, come up in numbers while a way into the root stood open. That way failed and no more came. Their children were born on the ground, had no way in the wood, and died at the stones when they hunted. Within a few generations nobody hunted the great ones. The great charges ran out, the stones went dark, and each host gathered again round the stones of its own smell. The villages emptied a hearth at a time, and no one generation saw it happen. Lately the Fraying elsewhere has torn a new way into the root, and cutters are coming up again.

## 1.8 What people here wrongly believe

- The born believe peopls are not persons, and that their own line was always of this ground.
- Arrivals believe they cannot die here.
- Everyone calls the great stones altars, the bait an offering, and the great feeders wardens or sovereigns set over the country. The names on the boss bars are this belief. The eight are animals with no self.
- Everyone says the things of the eighth night come down from the moon and that their slivers are pieces of it.
- Arrivals take the floor for stone.
- Arrivals say the islands are in the sky over the living ground. No height of building reaches them.
- The sand-country people believe wards are what bring feeders. By rules 4 and 5 that is half true.
- Many believe the eight kinds of stone and the eight nights of the moon are one design. They have nothing to do with each other.

## 1.9 What has no cause in the world

These belong to the game. Later text must not explain them.

The size of a piece of ground. How much a person can carry. Level numbers, critical hits, set bonuses and rarity colours. A great feeder taking more killing when more players fight it. Loot appearing at the feet. The compass and the mark where a player fell. Saving. The console and its cheats. A player's absence.
