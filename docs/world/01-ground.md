# 1. The ground

This chapter says what the world of the game is, where it stands among other worlds, and why each thing a player meets works as it does. Chapters 2 to 7 fill in the history, the places, the eight great feeders, the peoples, the materials and the creatures. Chapter 8 is an index for the people building the game. Until the naming pass, game things are called by plain description or by their internal keys.

Three standings are used in every chapter. A plain statement is firm for the people building the game. A statement given as somebody's telling or belief belongs to that somebody. A statement marked *proposed* follows from a cause here and is not in play yet; chapter 8 lists every one of them.

## 1.1 The world in one page

The game's ground is a world lying on a dead root.

The root belonged to an earlier world. Nothing made on this ground cuts it. Wood of this kind does one thing: it holds the ground over it steady. On other worlds a small piece of it is the dearest thing a household owns. Here it is the floor.

Three things follow from the root, and most of the game follows from them.

Ground here stays as it is put. A stone set in the air stays in the air.

The wood turns no animal. The things that eat at night come up in the dark all over this ground. Against them a household needs only the cheap part of a ward: a pot of the boiled-down remains of those same animals, and one trained person with a hand on the hearthstone until morning.

People who came up through the root are still joined to it. The way each of them came by stays open in the dead wood. A body beaten to the end of its strength drops into that way and comes out where it last lay down to rest. People born on this ground have no such way. They die where they are killed.

So the dangerous work here falls to outsiders. Those who came up through the root hunt. Those born here hold the hearths. The same fact bars the outsiders from the hearths: holding a ward through a bad night takes a body to the end of its strength, and at that point an outsider is gone from the stone. The born have a word for the outsiders. It is their word for persons with a sound left out, and in the game's English it is written *peopls*. The outsiders' trade is to kill feeders and boil them down, and above all to bait the strongest animal of a host onto old stone, kill it and boil it down. That trade is peopls craft. Every hearth on this ground is held with what it yields. The nights also worsen by it, because a host whose strongest is killed and left uneaten breaks into packs.

The players are peopls.

## 1.2 The frame this ground stands in

These are facts of the wider cosmos. The game does not alter them. `borrowed.md` lists each one and how firmly it stands.

**Worlds.** The Ashvattha, the Tree, bears the worlds. A world is a whole reality with its own ground, sun, moon and stars. Nothing lies beyond its sky and there is no space between one world and another. A world is never a planet. One world is reached from another along the Tree's wood, by roads that run in its roots.

**The speech and its failing.** Every world is held in being by a continuing speech, the Adi-Vak. In the present age that speech is failing. The failing is called the Fraying. Ground it reaches turns unsound. Two walkers pace the same field and get two lengths. A path walked before cannot be found again. Then the ground thins, and then it is gone. Nobody on this ground knows why the speech is failing, and no text of the game gives a reason.

**The wood.** The Tree is no part of the speech and does not fail with it. Worlds ended before the present ones, and their roots remain in the Tree as dead wood. Dead root-wood is black and cold and heavier than stone. Fire and rot do nothing to it. It holds the ground over it steady against the Fraying. Older and deeper wood holds harder and for longer, and wood that holds ground wears out in the end.

**Wards.** People made wards. No god gave them, and they are made on every world. A ward is four things.

- A housing of hard stone, with a hollow worn in the top for the hand and lines cut from that hollow down to the core and the charge.
- A core of dead root-wood, which steadies the ground.
- A charge of lees: the dark residue left when killed feeders are boiled down, packed in salt. While a hand drives it, feeders take the ground round it for the ground of something that kills their kind and does not eat them.
- A holder: one trained person with a hand on the stone, who drives the charge and pays for it in the body. The arm shakes, the palm is rubbed raw and there is a taste of metal in the mouth. A holder who drives past what the arm will take can lose the use of the hand, or die.

The rules of a ward are fixed, and the game uses them as they stand.

1. A ward turns away any feeder weaker than the strongest kill boiled into its charge.
2. A charge does not outlast the night. (A ward with a core of very old, deep wood can stand for a long time with no hand on it. Section 1.3 says why nothing on this ground does.)
3. Wood with no charge steadies the ground and turns nothing.
4. A charge that nobody is driving, or a spent one, is carrion to feeders. It draws them.
5. A held ward also draws feeders, as far as its edge, and that is where the fighting is.
6. People, weapons, arrows and fire cross a ward freely. So do feeders that never land and feeders that pass deep under the ground.
7. Holding is one person's work. The holder has to feel where the edge lies, and others standing with the holder add nothing. No word, song, gesture or gathering has any effect, on this ground or anywhere.

**Feeders.** The things wards were made against are the Rel'shari. The plain word is feeders, and the game's text uses the plain word. Their two low ranks have no self and no speech. The lowest, Kṣudhita, have no lasting shape. They form round whatever they are feeding on, and made things near them go soft: a fence post, a wall. The next rank, Jāgrat, hold a body and run in hosts. A trained fighter can kill a low feeder. Feeders with a self exist elsewhere. None is on this ground and none is to be written here.

Three rules of feeders carry much of the game.

1. A host goes where its strongest goes.
2. Among feeders a kill is eaten. One that kills and eats the strongest is followed in its place. When the strongest is killed by people, who do not eat it, nothing is followed and the host breaks into packs.
3. A weaker feeder stays off a stronger one's ground and eats there only on sufferance.

## 1.3 This ground

Everything from here on is about this ground, and most of it was made for the game. `invented.md` lists each thing and why the game needed it.

**Its place.** No living road reaches it. One dead road does, through the root it lies on. Where it hangs on the Tree is unknown to everyone on it. Arrivals say their own worlds are thinning, and that this is the one place they have stood where the ground was sound in every direction.

**The root.** The block the game calls `bedrock` is the root's upper face. Cores on other worlds are cut from wood that is already broken or has come to the surface. This root is whole, and no edge made here marks it. It is the largest piece of dead wood any crew has found. Whether it is wearing is not known to anyone.

The root steadies everything over it and turns nothing, by rule 3. A ward here needs no core. It also gets no help from the wood. A core is a piece cut free and seated in the housing, joined to the charge by cut lines. The root lies under rock and soil with nothing joining it to any pot. So a charge on this ground lasts one night and needs a hand, however old the wood beneath.

**Its size.** The root's upper face is wider than anyone has walked. A householder who walks the old roads for a lifetime sees the country a few hundred days across. The first comers built outward from the mouth for many generations (chapter 2), and nobody alive has walked past the last of their stonework. Whether the ground beyond is the same country with no stone on it, or whether there is an end to the ground, is not known to anyone. Firm, for the people building the game: the root is a root and has an end, and play never reaches it. No text or art shows an end of the ground. In play the old stonework goes on however far a player walks, and that belongs to the game (1.9).

**Three places on one root.**

- The living ground (dimension 0). A whole world with its own sky.
- The hollow (`dim:1`). The inside of the root, with the wood for floor and for roof, and rock, standing water and fungus between. It has no sky.
- The islands (`dim:2`). What is left of the world that grew on this root while the root lived. Its ground is gone except for islands in two layers. Its sky stopped at one dusk.

The hollow and the islands are remains of an earlier world, of the kind the wider cosmos calls Pūrvaśeṣa. In such remains the order of things is damaged. The hour of the day does not move. A fall off the last ground does not land: the faller is back on the last footing, hurt. Remains elsewhere are said to hold old feeders that fit neither low rank. None has been met in these, and none is to be written.

**The sky of the living ground.** A sun. A moon that rises as the sun sets and sets as it rises. Stars that turn. There are no seasons and no year. Sun, moon and stars cannot be reached.

A round dark the size of the moon crosses the moon's face in a round of eight nights. The moon is whole on the fourth night and wholly dark on the eighth. The round of eight nights is the only calendar. What the dark is cannot be found out from the ground. It is a fact of this sky, as the length of the day is. What it does on the ground has a cause, given in 1.6 under The dark night. The game now draws the dark on the fifth night of its count and holds the event on the eighth. The cause needs the two to fall on one night (chapter 8, changes).

**Down and up.** Digging ends at the wood. In play a body that gets beneath the floor comes in at the top of the sky and falls from there. That is given as a fact of this ground, and nothing else is built on it. Building upward reaches nothing.

**Leaving.** The two gates lead to the hollow and the islands, which are parts of the same root. The one way off is the dead road. It lies in the wood, and the place where it meets the root's upper face is called the mouth: the ground where every arrival first stood (the game's world spawn, described in 3.2). The road can be entered only by a pathfinder, and there is none on this ground. An arrival who left would lose the join to the root described in 1.6. Play follows nobody out.

## 1.4 Who is here

Chapter 5 treats these in full.

**Arrivals.** On every world, crews go into dead roots behind a pathfinder to cut cores. Few cutters live through many trips. This root cannot be cut. Crews that lose their pathfinder in it wander until they come up at its one mouth. Feeders live in the road, as they live in every root. A lone walker with empty hands is beneath their notice. A company is taken, and so is anyone carrying lees, wood or a full pack, since a load is what they come for. The ones who come up are the ones who dropped everything and walked on alone. That is why arrivals come up one at a time with nothing. The order of things is worse damaged in the road than anywhere a gate reaches, and crewmates who went in during the same week can come up many rounds of the moon apart. The players are these people.

**Householders.** People born here. They are few. Each is trained to hold a house ward and has a trade for the daylight. Most walk by day between the old houses and hold by night wherever they stop. No householder rules anything beyond a hearth. The game's townsfolk are householders, except `deserter`.

**The sand-country people.** People born here who do not boil down what they kill. They burn it to ash, so that nothing they store draws a feeder. Until the strongest burrower of their country was killed they lived on its ground, where nothing else came up. They are the people of the `ashen` event.

## 1.5 What Peopls Craft is

The born have a word for persons. Of those who come up at the mouth they say the same word with a sound left out, and they leave it out on purpose. The game's English writes it *peopls*. One of them is a *peopl*.

The born use the word of anyone who arrives alone, owns nothing, belongs to no household, and gets up again after a killing. It means one who looks like a person and does not die when killed. Many of the born hold that the ground puts peopls out at the mouth and that they were never born anywhere. Arrivals have taken the word for themselves.

*Craft* is the work the born cannot do and live: hunting feeders for their remains, baiting a host's strongest onto old stone, killing it and boiling it down. It covers everything that has a kill in it: lees, charges, forges, gates, crystal-work.

Peopls Craft is the name of the players and of what they do.

The word does work in play in three ways.

- **The division of labour.** The born hold and peopls hunt. All eight townsfolk wait for an arrival to supply them. None of them goes to a great stone. No arrival holds a hearth, for the cause in 1.6 under Rooms and townsfolk.
- **No ruler.** Peopls came singly and cannot be killed into obedience. Nobody born commands them and they have no chief of their own. A crew is people who agreed to work the same stones.
- **Two uses.** Householders say peopls craft of the trade they live by. They also say it of the dark stones and the bad nights, meaning that outsiders did it. The sand-country people use it only the second way.

The born descend from peopls. Their forebears came up at the same mouth, and the child of an arrival is born on the ground, has no way in the wood, and dies like anyone. The evidence lies in every old house: beds of the kind only an arrival uses, and stones cut to work on a way that no born person has. Householders have an answer for it. They say the forebears had peopls working for them, as householders do now. The answer suits them. A householder is paid as the owner of the ground, and that claim rests on forebears who were born of it. Chapter 2 says how the telling came to run that way without anyone deciding it should.

In play today the words appear only on the title screen. The text pass should put *peopl* in the mouths of townsfolk, as their ordinary word for the player.

## 1.6 The mechanics, in the order a player meets them

### Coming up with nothing

A new player stands on the ground over the mouth of the dead road with empty hands, for the cause in 1.4. Other players stand at the same place because the road has one mouth. A player who joins a world months old is a crewmate or a later crew, come up late for the same cause. They find the ground built on and the nights as bad as earlier kills have made them, because the hosts of a country are common to everyone in it.

### Breaking ground

Ground that lies over the root is set. It parts along even joints a stride apart and comes away in whole pieces. A piece put down stays where it is put, with or without anything under it. Cut the foot from a tree and the rest hangs. This is true in all three places. Living bodies, loose things, water and molten rock are not set. They fall and run as they would anywhere.

A tool lifts a piece whole only if the tool is as hard as the piece needs. A softer tool still breaks the piece, slowly, into nothing worth carrying. The `Needs a ... pickaxe` prompt is that rule. Chapter 6 says what the pick is working on.

### Making

Making by hand, at a `workbench` and at an `anvil` is ordinary carpentry, masonry and smithing. No pattern is a spell. The first steps of the Guide (wood, bench, pick, fire) are what every cutter knows from the trade and needs no telling. Chapter 6 covers every station.

### Food and ordinary animals

A body that cuts stone and fights gets hungry, heals when fed and wastes when starved. The animals that run from people are ordinary animals of this ground. They give meat, hide and feather. They pay no coin, because they are no feeders.

Nothing is tilled on this ground. The woodland kinds of feeder are bodied in green growth and feed on it, and a sown field is the thickest young growth there is. They come up in it at night. People here live on flocks gone wild, on hunting and on what grows without being sown.

### Night, light and feeders

Every hostile creature in the game is a low feeder, except the `ashen` fighters, who are people. Feeders come up out of the root. Nobody has seen one cross open ground to the place where it is found. It is found where it was not.

Light on a place stops feeders coming up there. Daylight does this, and so do a lamp, a torch, lit growth and standing crystal. So feeders come up at night under the sky, and at any hour under rock. That is why nothing hostile appears in block light, and why caves are never safe.

Light does not turn a feeder that is already up. One that came up in the dark walks into lamplight freely.

Kinds differ in what the sun does to them once they are up. Three kinds are bodied in green growth: `thornling`, `gelcap` and `bog_lurker`. Growth is made in the sun, and the sun does them no harm. They stay up through the day, rooted among bushes, sitting in grass or sunk in mud, and what a player meets at noon came up in the dark. Two kinds took their bodies where there is no sun, `gloomshade` in the hollow and the deep kind beneath it. Full sun undoes them. The low sun of dusk does not, so packs that set out at dusk arrive whole.

Low feeders on this ground are bodied in what they feed on. The woodland kinds are thorn and sprout, the sand kinds are grit, the kinds of the hollow are spore and fungus, the kinds of the islands are splintered crystal. One that nests in a store of iron is bodied in rust. What a feeder drops when it is killed is what it had been eating. That is why creatures drop wood, ore, bars and bone. Chapter 7 takes each kind.

### Coin

Coin is lees, weighed out. Every hearth needs it every night, so every householder takes it for anything. Feeders yield it, and ordinary animals do not. The game pays at the kill and leaves out the night over the pot, the salt and the fuel.

Unsalted lees is food to feeders and is soon eaten. Salted and sealed it lasts. On other worlds that is said to be a matter of weeks. Here a sealed jar is found good after many generations, for the cause that makes ground stay as it is put. Old chests hold it. Lees that lies in the ground with nothing to eat it does not go off either. It sets hard, and that is crystal (see Practice, the arm, crystal and totems).

### The way, the fall and the bed

A crossing along the Tree's wood carries the same person through whole, with their wounds. In living wood a way closes behind the walker as the wood grows. Dead wood does not grow. A way made through it stays open behind the one who made it, and to that one only. Every arrival came up through this root, and the root lies under all three places. So an arrival is never off the wood in which their own way lies open.

A living body holds itself where it stands. A body at the end of its strength, from a blow, a burn, hunger or a drop, stops holding, and the way takes it. It comes out at the near end alive: hurt, hungry, with the arm half spent and the good of any draught gone. This is what the game shows as YOU FELL and Rise again. A fall in the hollow or on the islands ends at the same bed, because it is the same wood.

**What is seen.** Somebody standing by sees the body go down and then sees bare ground. Nobody sees it leave, as nobody sees a feeder arrive. At the bed there is a person lying on it who was not there, breathing hard, as after a bad sleep. A householder in the next room hears nothing. The sand-country people have watched the first half of this from the far side of a fight, and it is the main reason they class peopls with feeders (5.3).

**The near end.** At first it is the mouth of the road. It moves to the place where the way's maker last lay down to rest, on the living ground. A body lying slack on a bed has let go a little of what a failing body lets go altogether, and the near end comes to it. The game marks the place with a `bedroll` and takes a hand laid on the bed, at any hour, for the lying down. The way was made onto the living ground and comes out only there, so a `bedroll` in the hollow or on the islands marks nothing. If the `bedroll` is destroyed the near end goes back to the mouth.

A body taken slack sheds what it carries loose. Lees is carried loose, by weight. About one part in seven is shed in the wood. Gear is strapped on and comes through.

The `recall_stone` is the same crossing made on purpose. It is crystal and cut lines round a gem of the deep rock. Driven by the hand it loosens its bearer for a moment, as feeder remains loosen whatever they touch (see Great remains), and the way takes them. The bearer goes awake and sheds nothing. The stone is warm afterwards and will not work again until it cools.

The limits are part of the fact.

- The way takes a failing body. Nobody has seen it bring back one that was already dead.
- It does nothing against age or sickness.
- It belongs only to those who came up through the root, and only on ground that lies on the root.
- The born have no way. Feeders have none. They die where they are killed, which is why a creature killed stays killed.

Death is final on this ground, as on every world. Arrivals believe they cannot die here. They are wrong, and the game never shows them the case.

A `guest_bed` is a holder's seat for the night and is offered to a householder. The arrival who offers it does not lie on it. No born person will sit through a night over the near end of a stranger's way, where a hurt peopl may be lying at any hour.

### Sleep

Nobody sleeps with a feeder close by or with packs at the walls. That the night is gone when a player wakes, and what several players must do for it to pass, belong to the game (1.9).

### Fire, and the ten hardnesses

`forge1` is a wood or coal fire in a ring of stone. It melts the soft ores of the living ground, fires sand and clay, cooks meat and burns logs to coal.

Matter that lies over the root is set harder the nearer it lies to the wood and the longer it has lain there. On the living ground hardness rises with depth: `wood`, `stone`, `iron`, then `lumenite` and `cinder` in the deep rock. The hollow lies between two faces of the wood, and its metals, `umbrite` and `noctrium`, are harder again. The islands are the oldest matter on the root, and theirs, `aether` and `prism`, are harder than those. Hardest is `eclipse`, in the lowest rock of the living ground, lying on the wood itself. The order in which each cuts the next was found by trial. Arrivals read it as an order of age, which is a guess.

The rock each metal lies in yields only to a pick of the hardness before it, or to anything harder. That is why a crew needs both gates before it can work its own floor.

From `cinder` upward no fire a person can light melts the ore. What does is given under Great remains.

### Why mine and make at all

A great feeder cannot be killed with wood. Each step in metal is taken to kill the strongest of the next kind of host. The remains of that kill are what work the next metal. Householders gather to whoever is doing this, because great lees charges a hearth against everything weaker than the animal it came from.

### The Guide

The Guide is the order of work. Its first steps are the cutter's own trade. From the first room onward it is the householders' telling: the order in which the born want the great stones taken, with how to make each bait. It passes among arrivals by word of mouth, and `wayfinder` has the fullest telling. It says what each killing opens. It does not say what each killing lets loose. Chapter 2 gives the cause of that silence.

### Rooms and townsfolk

Because the ground already lies on wood, a ward here needs no core. It needs a charge and a holder. A house ward on this ground is a pot of lees and a householder's hand on a hearthstone until first light. A householder on the road carries both: a clay pot, and a slab of hard stone with the lines cut in it.

A `guest_bed` in a room that is closed, has a door and a light, and is neither too small nor too large, is an arrival's offer to supply one hearth. The walls give the holder an edge to hold to. The light stops anything coming up inside. The door is there because a ward stops no person. One hand holds only so much ground, which is the limit on a room's size. The game shows the bed and the householder. *Proposed:* it also shows the pot and the slab beside the bed while someone lives there. The game leaves out the nightly charge. What a crew pays over the counter is what fills the pot.

While the room is occupied, no feeder comes up within 28 strides of it. That is the part of a house ward the game shows. A feeder that came up further off and is weaker than the kill in the pot is turned at the edge, and one that is stronger crosses. The game does not show the turning: a creature that came up outside walks in (chapter 8, changes). At night the householder stays within a step and a half of the bed, because the hand is on the stone. Break the bed and the householder walks on, and the ground round the house is open again. A player's own `bedroll` protects nothing: nobody is holding there.

**Why no arrival holds.** Every arrival grew up beside wards, and holding can be learned. What bars them is the way. On a quiet night a house ward costs a holder a sore arm. On a bad night, with a pack at the edge, it takes the holder to the end of their strength, and a born holder at that point holds on, shaking, until first light. An arrival at that point stops holding their own body, and the way takes them. They are lying at their bed, and the stone has a charge on it that nobody is driving, in the worst hour of the night. No household puts an arrival's hand on its stone, and no crew that understands this asks.

Each householder comes when there is trade for them: `wayfinder` to any ready room; `provisioner` when a crew is carrying more lees than its hearths use, which the game counts as forty coin on one person, since word of a surplus travels the old roads and carrying is that householder's trade; `smith` to an `anvil`; `tinker` and `runescribe` to their benches; `apothecary` to the fresh remains of `boss:1`; `beastcaller` to a crew that has killed the strongest of the shade-kind and can supply that kind's remains; `deserter` after a march of his own people has been beaten off. They buy anything, cheaply, because anything can be carried on to another hearth.

No householder will settle in the hollow or on the islands. A charge does not outlast the night and a holder rests at first light. Where the hour stands there is no first light, and holding that cannot end kills the holder.

Two held hearths are enough to draw packs (rule 5). A pack led by something stronger than the charge crosses it (rule 1), and people cross any ward (rule 6). So the protection round a home is gone for as long as `raid` or `ashen` lasts.

### Practice, the arm, crystal and totems

A person gets better at what they do. Levels and attribute points are the game's measure of that and nothing more.

The blue bar measures the arm. Driving anything through cut lines costs the body as holding a ward costs it. The bar shows how much more the arm will take before it stops answering. Nothing is stored in it. It comes back steadily whenever the arm is not driving.

Crystal is old lees. Lees that lies in the ground with nothing to eat it sets hard, as everything over the root does. Shut in rock it sets as a geode (`mana_ore`), found in all three places. In the open it sets as standing crystal, which gives light. On the `runeloom`, crystal is seated on wood with lines cut to it so that a hand can point it. A staff, driven, does to a body what feeder remains do to anything set: it loosens it.

A totem carries food. Two carved bones with raw remains of the shade-kind bound to them are unsalted, and unsalted remains are food. One of the lowest feeders, the kind with no shape of its own, forms on the food and takes its body from the totem's metal. It stays by the hand that carries its food, on sufferance, as any weaker feeder does beside a stronger. It goes for other feeders because they are food to it. It is gone when the bearer goes down.

Trinkets are small worked pieces. Some are crystal-work, some have remains in them, some are plain good gear. Each does one thing, and a second of the same adds nothing. Chapter 6 sorts them.

No two pieces of set metal work alike, and a thing taken from a feeder's body varies more, since the body was built of whatever the animal ate. That is the random prefix on gear, commoner on drops than on made things.

### The old stonework

Arrivals find the ground already built on. The history is in chapter 2.

- `wardstone` is the dressed stone of the first comers.
- A `village` is houses round a well, whole and empty. Householders on the road pass nights in them, which is why the crates are stocked.
- A `dungeon` is a sealed store: rooms cut under solid rock with no way in from above, holding lees, gear and made baits. `cache1` and `cache2` are the same work in the hollow and on the islands.
- A `ruin` is a small surface store that has been broken into.
- A `brood_nest` is a store where the salt failed. Unsalted lees is food, feeders came to it, and they have bred there since. This is why nests are found in old stonework and nowhere else.

### The great stones, the bait and the great feeders

The eight blocks `altar1` to `altar8` are great ward housings, cut by the first comers to carry a charge made from the strongest animal of a host. Such a charge, held, turned everything weaker than that animal from a wide country. Nothing made here breaks one. The paved floor, the pillars and the lamps round each are a killing floor.

Their charges were spent long ago. A spent stone is carrion of the kind last boiled into it (rule 4), and feeders are drawn to remains of their own kind as they are to any. So in each country the strongest animal of each kind of host lies at the stones that smell of its own. It has fed there, unhunted, for longer than any householder's telling reaches. That is why the great feeders are far larger than anything else on the ground. Their size is bulk, and a well-armed crew can kill one.

There are eight kinds of host. The ground has many hosts of each kind, one to a stretch of country. The game's flag for a first kill stands for the hosts of the country a crew lives in.

What the game calls an offering is bait: raw remains of that host's lesser animals, in a vessel made to reach one beast. A burrower is reached by a lure sunk in sand. A flier that never lands is reached by a kite. Smoke carries in the still air of the hollow. Shape, sound and gesture do nothing. The remains do the work. Four baits lie ready in old stores, and they still work, since a bait works by its material.

Laid on the stone, bait is a charge nobody is driving. The lesser animals leave it to the strongest, which comes up to eat. It eats the bait as it comes, so the bait is gone whether the crew wins or loses. While it is up, lesser feeders stay off its food and any `raid` or `ashen` attack breaks off. It stays while there is someone on the floor to fight. With nobody left it goes back down. No second great feeder comes up into another's fight.

The next animal to answer bait at stones of that kind is a different one: the strongest of the next host over, which has had the same long time unhunted and has moved onto ground left empty. It is as large as the first. Every forge, gate and bench needs its own remains, so crews go on calling them.

`boss:7` answers only at night, because the sun undoes its kind.

### What a first killing does

Three things are lost each time a crew first kills the strongest of a kind.

**The host.** Nobody ate it, so nothing is followed. The host breaks into packs over the whole country. Packs with nothing over them are food for every other kind, and what eats grows. In play every hostile creature hits harder and takes more killing after each first kill, and the creatures that come in attacks more so. Later kills of the same kind change nothing a crew can see: its country is already full of that kind's packs. One player's first kill is every player's.

**Its ground.** While it lived, lesser things stayed off its ground and fed where it allowed. Play shows this for three of the eight.

- `boss:1`. Its packs go for stores at dusk. This is `raid`. In play it can begin once this animal is dead, or once two householders are housed, since two held hearths draw by themselves (rule 5). It breaks at dawn. Stores of great remains, standing forges and standing gates draw as well. The game does not yet count them (chapter 8, changes).
- `boss:2`. Nothing but its own host came up on the burrower's sand, and the sand-country people lived there on those terms. It took some of them and they held that a lighter price than a pot. With it dead their country is open to every pack the crews have loosed. They march on the crews' houses at first light, when the shade-kind are off the ground and the night's charges are spent, and go home at dusk, because they hold no wards. This is `ashen`. They take lees from every store they break, to burn it, and a beaten march leaves that lees behind. Their fighters carry fired tokens (`ash_token`), which chapter 5 describes. A token charges no pot. No householder takes one in payment, and any of them will buy one for a little lees, as they buy anything. `deserter` deals in them. Once `boss:2` is dead a few sand-country fighters also come in behind the dusk packs. Two things here are not as the game plays today. The shade-kind and `cinder_wisp` come up on `desert` sand from the first night, and the fighters join `raid` after any two first kills. Both need changing for this cause to hold (chapter 8, changes).
- `boss:3`. See the next heading.

For the other five the game shows only the general hardening. Chapter 4 says what each held off.

**What it was made of.** A feeder's body holds what it fed on, and an old one's holds things found nowhere else. Eaten by another feeder, that would have passed on. Boiled down, it is lees, a furnace bed, a gate.

### The dark night

Under the hollow, in the deepest part of the root, lives the oldest kind of feeder on this ground: `moonspawn`, and its flier `lunar_howler`. This chapter calls it the deep kind. It took its body where there has never been any light, and it feeds on other feeders. Its body is lees set hard and pale, and it gives a pale violet light of its own. The slivers it carries (`moon_sliver`) are bits of that body.

A host goes where its strongest goes (rule 1). The strongest of the deep kind is drawn to the stones of its own kind (`altar7`), which stand on open ground under the sky. Light on a place stops feeders coming up there. For every other kind that means the sun or a lamp. For this kind the moon is enough. On seven nights of the eight the moonlit floor stops its strongest, and the host stays below. On the eighth night the moon is dark. The strongest comes up at its stones, and the host comes up behind it over the whole country round them, to feed on everything else that is up. The deep kind is never met in caves, dark as they are, because it goes only where its strongest goes. When it is up in numbers the night sky takes its colour. Other packs stay off the ground that night, so no `raid` falls on it. The kind has never had to go down before a dawn, and the sun undoes whatever is still standing.

For as long as any householder's telling reaches, none of this was seen. The strongest of the shade-kind, `boss:3`, held the deep rock under the surface as its ground and ate the host as it came up behind its strongest on each dark night. The strongest of the deep kind is the stronger animal, and it passed. With `boss:3` dead the deep kind comes all the way up, on every dark night, from the night after the kill.

Bait brings its strongest up under any moon, as hunger brings any animal into the open, and a few of its host come with it. That is why `boss:7` can be called on any night and why `moonspawn` are seen beside it.

On the night the moon goes dark, things the moon's colour are on the ground everywhere. The born say the moon is hollow that night and that what was in it has come down. The game's name for the event is their belief.

### Great remains: forges, the bench, gates

The lowest feeders make set things go soft, and on this ground every low feeder does. The remains of a great one go on doing it in small, before they are boiled down. On a ground where the wood sets everything hard, this is the one thing that loosens.

- **Forges.** `forge2` to `forge5` are each the remains of one great feeder in a ring of eight stones. Fire wakes the remains, and they loosen ore that no fire melts. A stronger animal's remains loosen harder ore. The ring bounds the working. A forge left burning goes on burning, so it works while its owner is away.
- **The bench.** The remains of `boss:1` are the weakest of the eight and loosen only made things. On the `tinker` bench two worked pieces are loosened far enough to be joined into one.
- **Gates.** A crossing needs a real relation to the far side, an opening, and the same person carried through. A gate kit is a frame of hard matter round the remains of an animal that took its body in the far place: `boss:3`, whose kind is bodied in the dark of the hollow, and `boss:5`, which is bodied in the pale stuff that hangs under the islands. The remains hold the relation and loosen the opening. Because the wood is under all of the living ground, a gate can be raised wherever its frame will stand. Pull a piece from the near frame and the remains come away and the opening shuts. The far mouth is only where the opening comes out. Nothing there is doing the work, so nothing there can be pulled down.

Stores draw. A forge is a store of great remains with no hand on it, and so are a gate and a chest of them.

### The hollow and the islands

Crews go there for the harder matter, for the great feeders at the first comers' stones, and for the sealed stores. Feeders are up at all hours in both, since neither has a sun. No pack or march comes there, because the stores are on the living ground.

### After the last

`altar8` stands on a raised base of dressed stone, ringed with lamps. The born call it a cairn and say the forebears closed their first and best stone under it. Nothing was closed. The eighth kind of stone is the largest housing the first comers cut, and the base under it held the store for its charge. The strongest of the stone-eating kind lies in that base and has eaten into it from beneath. It is bodied in dressed stone. The bait for it is a small housing with a charge on it that nobody drives, which is that animal's food, and it comes up through the base to take it. The deep kind falls in behind it during the fight, since it is the strongest thing present.

After its first killing no kind of host in the crew's country has a strongest that people did not kill. The crew holds the strongest kill there is. A charge made from it would turn everything left, and a great stone could be held again with it: the crew to make the charge and fight at the edge, and a householder to sit at the stone, which no householder has done in any telling. The game's present text calls a held stone lit. In householders' mouths *lit* means held, and *first light* is the hour a holder lifts the hand. Nothing in play makes that charge yet. The later systems start here: a great stone that can be charged and held, one night at a time, at the cost of going on killing.

## 1.7 The history, by source

Chapter 2 gives the history in full. This is the short form.

Nobody on the ground has dates for any of it. Arrivals know nothing of its past.

**The stonework.** Great stones of eight kinds at even spacings. The six kinds that stand on the living ground lie further apart as the host is stronger, as reach would fall if each was held with a charge of its own host's strongest. The two kinds in the hollow and on the islands lie closer than any, for a cause in 2.3. Sealed stores under rock. Vaults in the hollow and on the islands, so the first comers had gates. Made baits for four of the eight. Beds and stones that serve only a person with a way. The stonework shows a numerous people who were joined to the root, hunted at the stones and held a wide country. It does not show how that ended.

**What householders say,** chiefly `wayfinder`. Their forebears raised the stones and every one was lit. The hunters were lost at the stones, the great lees ran out, and no pot filled with small kills could turn what walked by then. Kill the great ones again and the stones can be lit again. This telling makes the ground theirs, the emptying a misfortune and the arrivals hired hands.

**What the sand-country people say,** as far as `deserter` tells it. The stones are what fattened the great ones. Every spent charge is carrion and every store draws. The first comers filled their own country with packs until nobody could hold it. Their own forebears walked out, burned their pots and went to live where a great one let nothing else in. This telling makes the pot the fault and burning the stores a duty.

**Firm.** The first comers were arrivals, come up in numbers over many generations while a way into the root stood open. That way failed and no more came. Their children were born on the ground, had no way in the wood, and died at the stones when they hunted. Within a few generations nobody hunted the great ones. The great charges ran out, the stones went dark, and each host gathered again round the stones of its own smell. The villages emptied a hearth at a time, and no one generation saw it happen. Lately the Fraying elsewhere has torn a new way into the root, and cutters are coming up again.

## 1.8 What people here wrongly believe

- The born believe peopls are not persons, and that their own line was always of this ground.
- The born believe the ground is the only ground there is, and that the forebears built to the ends of it.
- Arrivals believe they cannot die here.
- Arrivals believe this ground is small and its edge near, because the roots they know are broken pieces.
- Everyone calls the great stones altars and the bait an offering. Householders say each great feeder was set over its country by the forebears, and the names on the boss bars are that belief. The great feeders are animals with no self. Chapter 5 says who first laid offerings and why it worked.
- Everyone says the things of the dark night come down from the moon and that their slivers are pieces of it.
- Arrivals take the floor for stone.
- Arrivals say the islands are in the sky over the living ground. No height of building reaches them.
- The sand-country people believe wards are what bring feeders. In their own country that was true (5.3). Elsewhere it is half true, by rules 4 and 5.

## 1.9 What has no cause in the world

These belong to the game. Later text must not explain them.

The size of a piece of ground. How much a person can carry. Level numbers, critical hits, set bonuses and rarity colours. A great feeder taking more killing when more players fight it. Sand-country fighters growing stronger with each first kill. Loot appearing at the feet, and the trinket that widens it. Trinkets whose whole effect is a number. The compass and the mark where a player fell. Saving. The console and its cheats. A player's absence.

A torch or a lantern that burns without being fed, in a player's house and in a store sealed for generations. The night being gone when a player wakes, and the rule for several sleepers. An attack ending when the last player leaves the living ground. One great feeder at a time on the whole server. A great feeder that hangs in the air, and the sounds it makes. Where hot and cold country lie under one sun. The lack of rain. The old stonework going on however far a player walks. A first kill counting in every country a crew later walks to.

## 1.11 Open

- The way through dead wood never fails in play, so nothing shows its limits. The game never shows an arrival's death.
- No great stone is held in play. Everything in After the last is a starting point for later systems.
- The moon as drawn does not yet agree with the dark night (chapter 8, changes).
- What the dark on the moon is. Unknown to everyone on the ground, and left so.
- Whether the root is wearing. Unknown to everyone, and left so.
