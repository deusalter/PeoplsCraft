# Borrowed

The game's world stands inside the cosmos of an existing work of fiction. This file lists every element taken from that work's world. Only the frame is taken: how the cosmos works, the kinds of thing that exist, and the terms for them. No person, place, people or event of the source appears in the game, and the game is set on ground the source does not describe.

Borrowed elements may not be changed. Each row gives the element, how it stands in the source in this file's own words, how the game uses it, and what the game adds that the source does not say. Every addition is also a row in `invented.md`.

**Source standing** is one of three values.

- *author's own*: the source's author stated or approved it directly.
- *canon*: it is in the source's settled reference material.
- *unreviewed*: it is in the source's reference material and its author has not confirmed it. The game leans on these least, and they are the first to change if the source changes.

| Element | How it stands in the source | How the game uses it | What the game adds | Source standing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Worlds are whole, never planets | Each world is a complete reality with its own ground, sky, sun and stars. Nothing lies between worlds and nothing lies past a world's sky. | The living ground is one such world. No text or art shows space, another world in the sky, or an edge. | A sky with a moon that stands opposite the sun and darkens in a round of eight nights. A body that passes the floor comes in at the top of the sky. | author's own |
| The Ashvattha | The Tree that bears the worlds. Its roots and branches are the roads between them. It exists apart from the speech that holds worlds in being. | Named once as the frame. The game's ground lies on a dead root of it. | Nothing about the Tree itself. | canon, with the author's rulings behind its independence |
| The Adi-Vak | A continuing speech holds every world in being. A thing lasts while it is said. | Stated once, as the reason ground can fail and wood is valued. | Nothing. No game mechanic works by speech. | author's own |
| The Fraying | In the present age the speech is failing. Ground turns unsound, thins and is gone, the outermost places first. | The reason wards exist and the reason people value this ground. Lately it has torn a new way into the root. | The game gives it no cause and shows none of it on the living ground. | canon; the term appears in the author's ruling on wards |
| Dead root-wood | Wood from the dead roots of earlier worlds. Black, heavy, cold, proof against fire and rot. It holds ground steady against the Fraying. Crews cut it in dead and dangerous places, and it is rare and fought over. | The floor of the game's world is a whole dead root. | A root that is sound and so cannot be cut. A whole world lying on one. Ground that parts in even pieces and stays where it is put. Matter set harder near the wood. | author's own |
| The ward | Human-made, on every world. A carved stone housing, a core of dead root-wood, a charge of boiled-down feeder remains in salt, and one trained holder with a hand on the stone who pays for it in the body. | The model for every working in the game. Townsfolk rooms are house wards. The eight altars are great housings. | Wards with no core, because the ground already lies on wood. Great housings cut to carry the charge of a host's strongest. | author's own |
| No god gave wards | People developed them. Some faiths credit a god, and the trade does not. | The born and the arrivals both treat ward-work as people's work. | Nothing. | author's own |
| What a charge turns | A ward turns away anything weaker than the strongest kill boiled into its charge. | Why great lees are wanted, why a host's strongest is hunted, and why a pack led by something stronger crosses a house ward during a raid. | The reading of altar spacing as reach. | author's own |
| The life of a charge | A charge lasts one night. Spent or undriven, it smells like a dead animal and draws feeders. A held ward draws feeders to its edge. | Bait at an altar is an undriven charge. Stores draw raids. Two held hearths draw packs. Nobody holds where there is no dawn. | Bait vessels. Raids. The refusal of townsfolk to settle in the two other places. | author's own |
| Wood without charge | Wood alone steadies ground and stops no feeder. | The standing condition of the whole world. | The extension from a road post to a world. | author's own |
| What crosses a ward | People, weapons, arrows and fire cross freely. So do feeders that never land and feeders that pass deep underground. | Town protection ends when armed people march. The bait for the flier is flown and the bait for the burrower is sunk. | The march of the sand-country people. | canon, from the approved ward text |
| The house ward | Root-wood under a hearthstone and a pot of lees, held on bad nights by the householder with a hand on the hearth. | Every occupied room in the game. | The wood is left out, since the floor supplies it. The rules for a room: closed, lit, with a door, of a size one hand can hold. | canon, from the approved ward text |
| One holder; ceremony does nothing | A hold is one person's work. Nothing is done by many voices together, and no ceremony, wording or gesture has any effect anywhere. | A rule for all game text and all future systems. Offerings work by their material. | Nothing. | author's own |
| Lees | The dark residue left when feeder carcasses are boiled down, packed in salt. Unsalted, it is food to feeders. It is traded widely, and the lees of an old strong beast is worth a great deal. | The game's coin. Totems are unsalted charges. Nests are stores where the salt failed. | Lees as money. Crystal as old lees set hard. | canon, from the approved ward text |
| Rel'shari | The things that feed. The plain word in the source is feeders. They are not evil. Feeding is what they are. | Every hostile creature except the human fighters. Game text says feeders. | Their bodies on this ground, and the undoing of two kinds by sunlight. | canon |
| Kṣudhita and Jāgrat | The two low ranks. The first has no lasting shape and forms round each hunger, and it eats the lasting quality out of made things. The second holds a body. Neither has a self or speech. A trained fighter can kill one. Great size is not strength. | The game's creatures and its eight great feeders. | Great remains that go on eating the set of things: forges, the bench, gates. | canon |
| No higher feeder here | Feeders with a self are as strong as demigods, and no mortal beats one by force. | None exists on this ground and none may be written. | Nothing. | author's own |
| Might is the only order among feeders | They have no organisations of any kind. A host follows its strongest. | The eight bosses lead hosts and command nothing. | One strongest to a host for the whole ground. | author's own |
| Succession by eating | Whoever kills and eats the strongest is followed in its place. If the strongest dies uneaten at a mortal's hand, the host breaks into packs. | The cause of the difficulty step after each first boss kill, and of the three events. | Packs as food for other kinds, so that everything hostile hardens. | author's own |
| Ground and rank among feeders | The weaker stay off a stronger one's ground and eat where it allows. Old ones go back to where their own dead are. | Why a great feeder lies at spent stones of its kind, why few creatures come near a live boss, why no raid falls on the eighth night. | The rising that `boss:3` fed on. | canon |
| How low feeders come and go | Lamps and waking people hold them off. They are in one place and then the next, with nothing seen between. | The light rule for spawning. Nobody sees a creature arrive. | Feeders coming up out of the root. | canon |
| The rule of crossing | A crossing needs a real relation to the far side, an opening in that relation, and continuity. The same person arrives, wounds included. | Gates. The return at the bed. | A way through dead wood that stays open behind its maker. Remains that hold a relation to where an animal's kind came from. | canon |
| Cutting crews | Crews go into the dead remains of earlier worlds behind a pathfinder to cut root-wood. Few cutters make many trips. | The players are such crews, lost. | A root they cannot cut. One mouth. | author's own for the crews; canon for the pathfinder |
| A lone walker passes | On the roads in the roots, a single traveller is beneath the notice of what patrols there and a large party is taken. | Why arrivals come up one at a time. | Applied to one dead road only. | unreviewed |
| Pūrvaśeṣa | Remains of earlier worlds: real ground, still joined to living worlds by dangerous dead routes. The order of events is damaged in them. | The hollow and the islands. The fixed hour in both. The fall off the islands that does not land. Crewmates coming up seasons apart. | Which remains these are: the inside of this root and the world that grew on it. | unreviewed |
| New dead routes | As the Fraying goes on, old junctions fail and new ways tear open through dead remains. | Why cutters are coming up again after a long gap. | The gap. | unreviewed |
| A decline nobody sees | The thinning is slow enough that every generation adjusts and none calls it a disaster. | The manner in which the villages emptied. | Applied to a decline with a different cause. | canon |
| Death is final | The dead do not come back and nothing in the cosmos revises a death. | Stated as a rule of the game's world. The born and every creature die for good. Arrivals fall before death and are carried. | The limits on the way: it takes a failing body, never a dead one. | author's own |
| Power is open to anyone and is no fuel | Anyone can grow stronger. There is no stored energy and no meter in the world. | Levels are practice. The blue bar is read as the strain a driving arm will take, the holder's cost. | The bar remains a bar on the screen. | author's own |
| Terms and plain words | Ashvattha, Adi-Vak, the Fraying, Rel'shari, Kṣudhita, Jāgrat, Pūrvaśeṣa. The plain words feeders, holder, lees, edge. | Used with these spellings. Only the plain words appear in game text. | Nothing. | canon |
